# Deepali Vyas: The internal move playbook

Most professionals only become strategic about their careers when they decide to leave. Inside their own company, they coast, and hope to be noticed. Deepali Vyas says that's backwards, and it costs people years. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her playbook for moving up, or across, inside your current organisation.

## Why internal moves are different

First, why this matters. Vyas notes that companies increasingly prefer to fill roles internally. Internal hires tend to get up to speed faster, cost less to recruit, and often stay longer. So the opportunity is there. But few employees feel confident navigating an internal move. The gap, she argues, isn't opportunity. It's strategy.

And the challenge is different from an external job search. When you apply elsewhere, you get to tell your story fresh. Internally, your reputation is both your biggest asset and your biggest constraint. Colleagues have already formed a view of what you're capable of, and, crucially, of your ceiling. Your task is to change that view.

## The familiarity trap

Second, her most interesting insight. Familiarity can work against you. When people believe they already understand your range, they stop imagining you in a different role. The reliable medical writer who always delivers is seen as exactly that: a reliable medical writer. Not as the future head of a team, or as someone who could move into medical strategy or regulatory leadership.

So, in her view, advancing internally is less about proving more of the same, and more about deliberately expanding how people see you.

## Three common mistakes

Third, the mistakes she sees most often. Waiting to be noticed instead of making your ambitions visible. Treating the career conversation as a one-off event rather than an ongoing dialogue. And investing in your external image, like your LinkedIn profile, while neglecting your internal brand. Each seems minor, she says. Together they stall careers.

## The four-step playbook

Fourth, her playbook.

Step one: understand your internal brand, honestly. Your brand is what people say about you when you're not in the room. Being described as reliable, dependable or hard-working earns respect, but it doesn't necessarily signal that you're ready for more. Advancement usually requires being associated with strategic thinking, not just execution. A useful exercise is to ask a few trusted colleagues for three words that describe you, and see whether "strategic" or "leader" appears.

Step two: own the career conversation. Many people ask their manager, "What do I need to do to get promoted?" Vyas says that framing signals passivity: it hands all the power to the other person. Instead, state where you want to go, and ask for their perspective on how to get there. For example: "I'd like to move into a team lead role in the next year. What would you see as the biggest gaps, and where could I start showing that?" That shifts the conversation from evaluation to alignment.

Step three: expand your visibility beyond your immediate team. Senior promotions and internal moves are usually decided by several people, many of whom may not know you. If they don't know your work, you're not in the conversation. Cross-functional projects, presenting to other departments, and building relationships with leaders in the area you want to move into all help.

Step four: treat it as a campaign, not a single request. One conversation won't change how people see you. Consistent visibility, contribution and positioning over around sixty to ninety days will.

## The readiness check

Fifth, before you ask for an internal move, she suggests three questions. Do you have advocates outside your immediate team who would speak up for you? Have you made your ambitions visible to the people who decide? And have you delivered work that goes beyond your core function? If the answer to any of them is no, she advises building those conditions first, rather than asking too early.

She also offers a direct script for when you're ready, from another issue: "I'd like to be considered for this role in the next few months. What would it take to get there?" Name the role, name the timeframe, and ask. Silence, in her words, costs opportunities.

## Connecting the dots

These ideas connect to the rest of her playbook. Your BRAC book provides the evidence. Sponsors provide the advocacy in rooms you're not in. And the internal move conversation, held early and repeated, turns your manager into a partner rather than a gatekeeper.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that people should be as strategic about internal moves as they are about external job searches. Familiarity is a trap: colleagues stop imagining you in a bigger role, so you must deliberately expand how they see you. Understand your internal brand, state where you want to go rather than asking what to do, build visibility beyond your team, and run it as a sixty-to-ninety-day campaign.
