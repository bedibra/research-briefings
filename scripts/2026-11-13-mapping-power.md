# Mapping power in your organisation

Every company has two organisation charts. One is on the intranet: boxes, lines and titles. The other is invisible: who people actually go to for advice, who people trust, and whose opinion quietly decides things. If you only know the first chart, you'll keep being surprised by decisions. In this episode, we'll learn how to map the real power structure in your organisation, and how to use that map to get things done.

## Formal versus informal power

First, the idea. Formal power comes from your position: your title, your budget, your authority to approve or reject. Informal power comes from other sources: expertise, relationships, trust, access to information, and reputation.

In 1993, David Krackhardt and Jeffrey Hanson published a classic Harvard Business Review article called "Informal Networks: The Company Behind the Chart". They compared the formal organisation to a skeleton and the informal organisation to the central nervous system. The skeleton shows the structure, but the nervous system is what actually moves things.

## Three networks to map

Second, Krackhardt and Hanson suggested looking at three different networks, because each reveals a different kind of power.

The advice network. Who do people go to for expertise and problem-solving? In medical writing, it might be the senior writer everyone asks about ICH guidelines, or the statistician whose opinion on the analysis ends every debate. These people shape day-to-day decisions, regardless of their title. According to the article, this network matters most when you're making routine operational changes.

The trust network. Who do people confide in? Who do they share sensitive information with, and who do they rally behind in a crisis? Trust networks often explain why some changes succeed and others meet mysterious resistance. They matter most during major change or a crisis, like a reorganisation.

The communication network. Who talks to whom about work, day to day? This network shows how information flows, and where it gets stuck. Gaps here often explain why teams duplicate work or miss important updates.

The same person can be central in one network and peripheral in another. A director might have formal authority but sit outside the trust network. A mid-level scientist might have no direct reports but be the hub of the advice network.

## How to build your own power map

Third, a practical method. You don't need a survey or software. You need a notebook and good observation.

Start with a specific goal. Don't try to map the whole company. Pick a decision or initiative that matters to you. For example: getting a new review process adopted across clinical and regulatory teams.

List the stakeholders. Write down everyone who influences, approves, implements or could block it. Include people outside the obvious chain: the programme manager, the influential senior writer, the executive assistant who controls the vice president's calendar.

Place each person on a simple grid. A widely used stakeholder tool plots people by two dimensions: how much influence they have, and how much they care about your issue. High influence and high interest: these are your key players, and you need to engage them closely. High influence but low interest: keep them satisfied and informed, but don't overload them. Low influence but high interest: keep them involved, they can be valuable allies. Low on both: keep them informed.

Then add a third layer: each person's likely stance. Are they a supporter, neutral, or a likely opponent? And what's their underlying interest? What would make this a win for them?

Finally, trace the connections. Who listens to whom? Who trusts whom? Often the best way to influence a senior decision-maker is through the person they trust most, not through a direct pitch.

## Clues that reveal informal power

Fourth, how do you spot informal power? Watch for these signals.

In meetings, notice whose comments change the direction of the discussion, and who people look at before they answer a question.

Notice who gets copied on important emails, and whose approval people seek informally before making a formal proposal.

Notice who gets invited to the small pre-meetings, where real decisions often happen.

Notice who's consulted when there's a crisis. That's usually the trust network in action.

And notice who long-tenured employees respect. People who've been around for many years often know exactly where influence sits.

## Using your map

Fifth, using what you've learned. Engage key players early, privately, before formal meetings. Use connectors, people central to the advice or trust networks, to help spread support for your idea. Address the concerns of potential opponents directly, rather than hoping they won't notice. And keep updating your map, because power shifts with every reorganisation, new hire and leadership change.

One important caution: use your map to build relationships and understanding, not to manipulate. The goal is to make good decisions happen, with people on board.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, many large companies now use organisational network analysis, often called O-N-A, based on collaboration data, to identify hidden influencers and overloaded collaborators. Whether or not your company does this, the principle is the same: the informal network matters, and leaders who understand it get more done.

## Recap

To recap. Every organisation has a formal chart and an informal one, and the informal one often decides. Map three networks: advice, trust and communication. For any goal, list stakeholders, place them by influence and interest, note their stance and interests, and trace who listens to whom. Then engage early, use trusted connectors, and keep your map up to date.
