# Deepali Vyas: Performance is the entry fee, not the prize

Here's an uncomfortable idea: doing excellent work is not what gets you promoted. It's what keeps you in the running. Welcome to a special series of Research Briefings, an unofficial digest of the career ideas of Deepali Vyas, the executive recruiter who calls herself "The Elite Recruiter". In this first episode, we'll look at her most central argument, that performance is the entry fee to the corporate game, not the prize.

## Who Deepali Vyas is

First, some context on the source. Deepali Vyas has spent around twenty-five years in executive search, including seven years at Korn Ferry, the global recruiting firm. She says she has reviewed more than a million résumés and conducted over fifty thousand interviews. Today she runs a large following on YouTube, LinkedIn and TikTok, a weekly newsletter called The Elite Edge, and a podcast. Her signature phrase is "the corporate truth", and her signature metaphor is that careers are a game of chess, not checkers. This series summarises her ideas in our own words, drawn from her videos and newsletter. It's not produced by her or affiliated with her.

## The client who did everything right

Second, the story she uses to explain the problem. In one newsletter, Vyas describes a senior manager with eight years at her company and consistently strong reviews. She was passed over for promotion again. The person who got the role had half her tenure, and, in Vyas's telling, roughly half her output. Her diagnosis was blunt. The client wasn't doing anything wrong. She was playing the wrong game.

Vyas calls this the "bare minimum myth": the belief that excellent results will, on their own, get you noticed and rewarded. She describes high performers who stayed at the same level for four years, waiting to be discovered. Her point is that people are rarely overlooked because they lack skill. They're overlooked because the right people don't know what they've done.

She uses a marathon analogy. Everyone who finishes a marathon runs the same twenty-six miles. The winners train, pace and make decisions during the race differently. At work, everyone does the baseline job. Those who get promoted also make their work visible, build relationships with decision-makers, and start operating at the next level before the title arrives.

## Why less qualified people get promoted

Third, her explanation of a familiar frustration: watching someone less qualified move ahead. Vyas says it usually comes down to three behaviours, and none of them is luck.

They manage up. They build real relationships with the people who make promotion decisions.

They make their work loud. They send updates nobody asked for, recap their wins, and translate results into business outcomes in language leaders understand.

And they volunteer for visibility. Cross-functional projects, executive presentations, even the unglamorous assignments nobody else wants.

She's careful to say none of this requires being fake or political in the negative sense. It's about not leaving your reputation to chance.

## The three truths about promotion

Fourth, a framework from one of her newsletter issues, which she frames as three truths.

Truth number one: hard work is expected, not the differentiator. Quiet consistency often goes unnoticed. Her suggested habit is a two-minute weekly email to your manager covering what you accomplished, what you're focused on next, and any support you need. She describes it not as bragging, but as documentation and leadership maturity.

Truth number two: promotions require political capital. Decisions often happen in rooms you're not in, where people advocate for names they remember and trust. Her self-check is simple. Can you name at least two people outside your reporting line who would vouch for your impact today? If not, your circle is too small.

Truth number three: your manager may never be your sponsor. Some managers are insecure, disengaged, or simply lack the clout. So Vyas says you must manage up and around, and treat your career like a campaign.

## Performance versus readiness

Finally, a distinction that ties it together. Vyas separates performance from readiness. Performance means meeting the bar for your current role. Readiness means you're already operating at the next level. Her question is a sharp one: are you doing your job, or the job you want? Promotion committees, she argues, don't reward the best person at the current level. They promote the person they can already picture in the next one.

She also stresses asking directly. Don't assume people know you want a promotion. Name a target role and a timeframe, and ask what it would take to get there. In her words, clarity creates momentum, and silence costs opportunities.

## What to watch next

In the episodes that follow, we'll go deeper into each piece of her playbook: sponsors and the hidden org chart, how to document your wins, the meeting before the meeting, executive presence, the hidden job market, and how to protect yourself when layoffs are being planned.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that performance is the entry fee, not the prize: it keeps you in consideration but rarely wins the promotion. People who move up manage up, make their work visible, and volunteer for high-exposure work. And the shift that matters is from performing well in your current role to visibly operating at the next one, and asking for it out loud.
