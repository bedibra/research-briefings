# Deepali Vyas: The best jobs never hit the job boards

By the time a great job appears online, there's often already a frontrunner. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her view of the hidden job market: how hiring really happens, what the data says, and her step-by-step method for finding roles before they're posted.

## How hiring really happens

First, the five stages she describes. Stage one, a need appears: someone leaves, a new initiative is funded, or a new executive arrives and wants to build a team. Stage two, the hiring manager asks, "Who do we know?", and thinks first of people they've worked with or who come recommended. Stage three, internal and referred candidates are considered, because they're faster, cheaper and less risky. Stage four, for senior or hard-to-fill roles, recruiters tap their networks. Vyas says that's exactly what she does: she asks trusted contacts for the best person they know. And stage five, the posting goes up. Sometimes it's real. Sometimes it's a formality. And sometimes there's no active job behind it at all.

Her point is that you have the most influence in stages two to four, before the job is public.

## What the data says

Second, the numbers. Interestingly, Vyas pushes back on the popular claim that seventy to eighty percent of jobs are never posted, saying there's no solid study behind it. Instead, she cites recruiting-platform data. According to the recruiting software firm Gem, referrals make up under two percent of applications but around seventeen percent of hires. Internal candidates are a tiny fraction of applications but over twelve percent of hires. Data from another platform, Ashby, suggests referred candidates reach interview about forty percent of the time, compared with about twelve percent for cold applicants. And according to CareerPlug, job boards produce about sixty percent of applications but fewer than forty percent of hires. Finally, Greenhouse data suggests roughly one in five postings has no active hiring behind it. These are vendor figures, mostly from the United States, but the pattern is consistent: hiring managers reduce risk by favouring people they know, or people someone vouches for.

## Six unwritten rules

Third, her rules of the hidden market. People are hired because they're remembered, not just because they're qualified. Build relationships before you need anything. Give the people who refer you a short story about you that they can repeat. Recruiters can only find you if your profile uses specific titles, skills and outcomes. Weak ties, acquaintances rather than close friends, often open more doors. And helping others with introductions compounds over time.

She also lists signs you've become invisible: most of your search is online applications, you've had no conversations outside your company in thirty days, no recruiter has contacted you in six months, your LinkedIn headline is only your job title, and your entire network works at your current employer.

## The method: finding jobs before they're posted

Fourth, her playbook, in five steps.

Step one, define your lane. Write down your target industry, function, next level, location and company stage. She notes that people from large corporations often fit best in growth companies that are professionalising and hiring experienced leaders.

Step two, follow the money. Companies that have just raised funding are about to hire. She suggests tracking funding announcements through tools like Crunchbase, free funding newsletters or Google Alerts, and building a target list of about twenty-five companies. For big companies, the equivalent signals are new executive hires, acquisitions, priorities named on earnings calls, and new sites or regions.

Step three, read the signals. For each target, look at what they say they'll spend the money on, which roles are open, which leadership roles are missing, and who joined recently. Then write one sentence: "I think they need this, because of that."

Step four, use the investor back door. Venture capital firms often have talent partners and portfolio job boards. One good relationship there can connect you to dozens of companies.

Step five, find the right person. In small companies, that's usually the founder. In mid-sized ones, the hiring leader plus the head of talent. In large ones, the hiring leader, a peer on the team, and the internal recruiter.

## Applying it in pharma

Fifth, how this translates to pharma and biotech. The same logic applies. A biotech that has just closed a big funding round, or a company that has just licensed a new asset, will soon need people in clinical, regulatory and medical writing. A pharma company that names a new therapeutic-area priority on its earnings call will build teams around it. Following those signals, and reaching the hiring leader early, puts you in stage two of the process instead of stage five.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that the best jobs are often filled through relationships before they're posted, and the data on referrals and internal hires backs up the pattern. Define your lane, follow funding and strategic announcements, read what each company is likely to need, and find the person who'll actually make the hire.
