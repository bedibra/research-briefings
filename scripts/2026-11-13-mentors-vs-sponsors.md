# Mentors versus sponsors

A mentor talks to you. A sponsor talks about you. That one sentence captures a distinction that quietly shapes who gets promoted in almost every large company. In this episode, we'll look at the difference between mentors and sponsors, what the research shows about why sponsorship matters so much, and how to earn a sponsor, because you can't simply ask for one.

## The difference

First, the definitions.

A mentor gives you advice, perspective and support. They help you think through a tricky stakeholder, prepare for a presentation, or decide whether to take a new role. Mentors can be anywhere in the organisation, or outside it. The relationship is mostly private, and the mentor takes very little risk.

A sponsor is different. A sponsor is a senior person with real influence who uses their own credibility to advance your career. They put your name forward for a stretch assignment. They argue for you in the calibration meeting where ratings are decided. They say "she's ready" or "he should lead this" in rooms you're not in. A sponsor takes a risk on you, because if you fail, it reflects on their judgement.

So mentoring is about development. Sponsorship is about advancement.

## What the research shows

Second, the evidence.

The landmark work here is a 2010 Harvard Business Review article by Herminia Ibarra, Nancy Carter and Christine Silva, titled "Why Men Still Get More Promotions Than Women". It drew on surveys by the research organisation Catalyst of more than four thousand high-potential professionals who had graduated from top business programmes. A striking finding: women were at least as likely as men to have mentors. Yet in the follow-up, the men had received more promotions. The difference was in what the mentors did. Men's mentors were more often senior and influential, and more often actively sponsored them, pushing for their next role. Women tended to receive advice. Men tended to receive advocacy. The authors' conclusion was that women were over-mentored and under-sponsored.

The economist Sylvia Ann Hewlett, who founded the Center for Talent Innovation, spent years researching sponsorship, and summarised it in her book "The Sponsor Effect". Her research reported that a woman manager with a sponsor was around nineteen percent more likely to be promoted to executive ranks than one without. And interestingly, her work suggests sponsorship benefits sponsors too: senior people who sponsor rising talent were themselves more likely to be promoted, because building strong successors is a mark of leadership.

The lesson applies to everyone, not only women. Sponsorship is often the hidden factor in promotions, and people who don't know it exists are at a disadvantage.

## Why you can't simply ask

Third, a key point. You usually can't walk up to a vice president and say "will you be my sponsor?" Sponsorship is earned, because the sponsor is betting their reputation. They need to trust two things: that you're excellent, and that you're loyal, meaning you'll deliver and make them look good.

Think of it as an exchange. The sponsor offers influence and opportunities. In return, you offer outstanding performance, reliability, and support for their goals. Hewlett described the protégé's side of the deal as delivering results and bringing something distinctive to the sponsor's team.

## How to earn a sponsor

Fourth, the practical steps.

Step one: deliver, visibly. No one sponsors average work. Excellent performance on work that matters to senior leaders is the foundation. In pharma, that might be the pivotal study report, a high-stakes submission, or rescuing a delayed project.

Step two: identify potential sponsors. Look one or two levels above your manager, ideally people who sit in talent discussions: department heads, directors in neighbouring functions, or the leader of a programme you work on. Ask yourself: who has influence over the decisions that affect my career?

Step three: get on their radar through work, not flattery. Volunteer for a project they care about. Present clearly in a meeting they attend. Offer to help with one of their priorities, perhaps a process improvement or a team initiative. Let them see your work first-hand.

Step four: make your ambitions known. Sponsors can't advocate for a goal they don't know you have. When the moment is right, say it clearly: "I'd like to move into a principal role in the next eighteen months. What would you need to see from me?"

Step five: make it easy and safe for them. Follow through on everything. Keep them informed. Never surprise them with bad news. Every time you deliver, you repay their risk, and their confidence grows.

Step six: use your mentors as a bridge. Good mentors can turn into sponsors, or introduce you to people who might sponsor you. Ask a trusted mentor: "Who do you think I should get to know, and would you be comfortable introducing me?"

## Being a sponsor yourself

Fifth, the other side. As you become more senior, sponsoring others is one of the most powerful things you can do, both for them and for your own reputation as a leader. Notice people whose talent isn't being seen. Put their names forward. Give them visible assignments. This is also one of the most practical ways to build a more diverse and fair organisation.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, many companies are formalising sponsorship through structured programmes that pair high-potential employees with senior leaders. If your company has one, find out how people are selected and how to get nominated. If it doesn't, the informal version is still available to anyone who builds the right relationships.

## Recap

To recap. A mentor talks to you, a sponsor talks about you. Research from Catalyst and Sylvia Ann Hewlett shows that sponsorship, not just mentoring, drives promotions. Sponsors are earned through excellent, visible work, clear ambitions, and reliability. And as you grow, sponsoring others is a hallmark of real leadership.
