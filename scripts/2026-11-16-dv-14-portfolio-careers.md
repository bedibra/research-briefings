# Deepali Vyas: Portfolio careers and consulting collectives

"Your next career chapter probably won't come from a job posting. It'll come from a business model." That's how Deepali Vyas closes one of her videos. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her argument that corporate professionals should build a portfolio career, or at least understand their options, long before they need one.

## Why she says security has changed

First, the context. Vyas often points to news like large technology companies offering buyouts or cutting staff while still reporting strong profits. Her lesson: a successful company and a secure job are two different things. Your employer can value you highly and still eliminate your role, because of budget, structure, geography, automation or investor expectations. It's not always a judgment on your talent. But the impact on your career is the same if you have no alternatives. Another of her videos is titled "The nine to five is dying. Here's what comes next."

Her message isn't to panic or quit. It's that waiting for certainty is usually how people lose leverage. As she puts it: your company has a strategy for its future, and you need one for yours. Play chess, not checkers.

## What a portfolio career is

Second, the definition. A portfolio career simply means you don't rely on one job or one income stream. You build a portfolio of work: consulting, advisory roles, fractional leadership, meaning part-time executive roles, content, speaking, or board seats. Some people do this alongside a full-time job. Others make it their main career.

## Three questions to start

Third, her three starting questions for anyone with years of corporate experience.

One: can your experience become another source of income? What do people constantly ask you for help with? What have you built, fixed or scaled? Be specific about who would pay for that, and test demand with real conversations before taking any leap.

Two: can you describe your work as business value? Don't define yourself by your tasks. Explain what changes when you do them. Do you increase revenue, reduce cost, reduce risk, speed things up, or help leaders make better decisions? The more clearly you link your work to an executive priority, the more portable your value becomes.

Three: can you widen your search? Someone with years in technology might find their next opportunity in healthcare, manufacturing or insurance. A company in a different industry may be struggling with a problem you already know how to solve. For a pharma professional, that might mean medical communications agencies, health technology companies, contract research organisations, or regulatory consulting.

And throughout, build relationships before you need them. Reconnect with former colleagues and clients, and tell them what you're exploring.

## Consulting collectives

Fourth, a model she says is quietly exploding among former corporate high performers: the consulting collective. It's a small group of independent operators who each run their own business, but team up for bigger, more complex client work. Each keeps their own company, their own rates and their own clients. When a multi-disciplinary project comes along, they bid together, deliver together and split the revenue. She jokingly calls it the Avengers model for grown professionals.

Why does she like it? Solo consulting can be lonely, big clients are wary of one-person shops, and agencies take away your autonomy. A collective offers independence plus scale. And clients increasingly want combinations that one person rarely covers: strategy plus execution, business plus technology, AI plus operations.

Her advice for setting one up is very practical. Pick complements, not clones: if you're strategy, partner with execution; if you're scientific, partner with commercial. Start with no more than three people, because more becomes a governance headache. Run a single pilot project, with one client and one scope over about six months. Keep everything else separate: your own clients stay yours. Decide revenue splits based on roles, such as who sells, who delivers and who runs operations, not on feelings. And put it all in writing: who owns the client relationship, how revenue is split, how someone exits, and who owns the intellectual property. Real agreements, not a shared document.

## Following talent, not passion

Fifth, one more related theme. In a video titled "Follow your talent, not your passion", Vyas argues that sustainable careers, inside or outside corporate life, are built on what you're genuinely good at and what the market will pay for. Passion can grow from mastery. That's a useful filter when deciding which parts of your experience could become a portfolio.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that a profitable employer doesn't guarantee a secure job, so professionals should understand their options before they need them. A portfolio career spreads your income across consulting, advisory work, fractional roles, content or boards, and starts with what people already ask you for. And consulting collectives, small teams of complementary independents with clear written agreements, are one of the cleanest ways she sees experienced people make that model work.
