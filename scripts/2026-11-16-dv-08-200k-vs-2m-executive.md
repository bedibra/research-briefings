# Deepali Vyas: The two-hundred-thousand professional versus the two-million executive

Why does one person earn two hundred thousand dollars a year and another earn two million? According to Deepali Vyas, it's not that the second person is ten times smarter, or works ten times harder. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her idea of "altitude", and what changes as you move from professional to executive.

## Output versus outcomes

First, her core distinction. The professional is paid for output: a delivered deck, a report, a finished project. The executive is paid for outcomes: revenue protected, risk avoided, markets entered, talent retained. The gap between them, she says, isn't talent, luck or hours. It's altitude.

She contrasts the two across several habits. The professional brings answers. The executive brings better questions. The professional talks about effort. The executive talks about impact. The professional waits for complete data. The executive decides at around seventy percent certainty and owns the call. The professional fills the calendar with meetings. The executive protects time to think. The professional networks with peers. The executive networks with sponsors, boards and the market. The professional negotiates raises. The executive negotiates scope and equity. And the professional is known by their team, while the executive is known by recruiters before they ever apply.

Her challenge: look at your calendar honestly. Which column does it reflect?

## Six unwritten rules

Second, her rules of altitude.

Hard work has a ceiling; leverage doesn't. Professionals scale by adding hours. Executives scale through people, systems and decisions.

If you can't put a number on it, it didn't happen. "I led the migration" is a task. "The migration saved three million a year and took six weeks off the financial close" is a business case.

You're paid for the problems you absorb. Ask yourself who calls you when things go wrong.

Competence gets you hired; judgment gets you promoted. Senior judgment means knowing what to ignore, when to escalate, and when to say no.

The market sets your price, not your manager. Your manager controls a three percent merit increase. The market controls your next big jump.

And executives are selected, not developed. They start operating at the next level before the title arrives.

## The director to vice president shift

Third, a related idea from another issue, about moving from director to vice president. Vyas says it's a different job, not a bigger version of the same one. Directors are rewarded for being the expert who solves problems in depth. Vice presidents are rewarded for building systems that produce answers without them: setting direction, developing people, taking a longer view, and speaking in business outcomes.

The trap is that directors who keep proving they're the best director in the room get passed over. Her self-check: in your last three conversations with executives, did you talk about what you did, or about what your team is building and where it's heading?

## Five tools to gain altitude

Fourth, her practical tools.

Tool one: a calendar audit, about fifteen minutes. Tag the last two weeks of meetings as either execution time, meaning updating, reporting and attending, or executive time, meaning deciding, strategy, relationships and developing others. Then shift the balance: delegate one recurring meeting, decline one status update, and use the time for one conversation that moves the business.

Tool two: the enterprise math rewrite. Translate your wins into what I did, what changed, and what it was worth.

Tool three: a three-move altitude plan before year end. Own one stalled decision and communicate the decision, the reasoning, the risk and the next step. Get into one higher-level room, like a quarterly business review or steering committee, with a point of view. And have one real business conversation with a leader above you.

Tool four: the "scope, not raise" conversation. At your review, ask which problems you should own next year and what success would look like. Then discuss pay based on that bigger scope, and get it in writing.

And tool five: the market test. Has a recruiter contacted you in the last ninety days? Would someone searching your name understand your value in ten seconds? Could three people outside your company describe your strengths? Two or more no's, she says, means your value is invisible to the market.

## Ownership

Finally, a note on where the biggest pay packages come from. Citing Economic Policy Institute research on chief executive pay, Vyas observes that large packages rest mostly on ownership, usually equity. Setting the fairness debate aside, her question for listeners is: what do you own? Equity, a budget, a portfolio of clients, a product, an audience, or a side business?

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that professionals are paid for output and executives for outcomes, and the gap is altitude, not intelligence or hours. Executives decide with incomplete data, protect thinking time, put numbers on their impact, and negotiate scope rather than raises. And you don't get promoted into altitude: you start operating there, and the title follows.
