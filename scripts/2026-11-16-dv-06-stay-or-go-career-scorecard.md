# Deepali Vyas: Stay or go? The career scorecard

One bad review. One tense conversation with your boss. One week where you feel invisible. And suddenly you're scrolling job sites at midnight. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her tool for deciding whether to stay, push for promotion, or start planning your exit, and why she says the decision should be strategy, not reaction.

## The problem: deciding on mood

First, the problem as she frames it in a recent video, "Time to change jobs?". The hardest question she gets asked isn't simply "should I quit?". It's how to know whether to stay and invest more effort, push for a promotion, or begin planning an exit. And her honest observation is that most people make that decision based on how they feel that week. A disappointing review, a difficult meeting, a moment of feeling overlooked. That's not enough information for a decision that shapes years of your career.

## The five-dimension scorecard

Second, her solution: what she calls the career scorecard, a system she says she developed over twenty-five years in executive search. You rate your current role on five dimensions, each from one to three. The point is to bring some objectivity into a conversation that's usually purely emotional.

Dimension one is money. Am I paid fairly for the value I create? Not what the market pays on average, but what someone with my experience and results should earn.

Dimension two is culture. Does this environment let me do my best work? Or do I spend more energy on political games than on the work itself?

Dimension three is development. Am I still learning, expanding my scope, and building career capital? Or have I stopped growing in this role?

Dimension four is sponsorship. Is there someone with real influence here who mentions my name in rooms I'm not in? Vyas stresses this one, because, as she puts it, performance alone doesn't get you promoted. Sponsorship does.

And dimension five is connections. Is this role expanding the quality and range of my professional network? Or have I become more isolated than when I started?

## Reading the score

Third, how to interpret it. A low score on one dimension is worth noting, but it isn't a reason to leave on its own. Every job has weak spots. What matters is the pattern. Low scores across several dimensions, she says, tell you where career risk is building up.

You can also use the scorecard to decide what to fix. If money is low but development and sponsorship are strong, the right move might be a well-prepared compensation conversation rather than a job search. If sponsorship and connections are low, the fix might be building relationships across the organisation, which we covered in earlier episodes. If culture and development are both at one, and nothing you do seems to change them, that's a stronger signal to go.

## Leave from strength, not frustration

Fourth, the questions she says to ask before making any move. What leverage have I actually built? What evidence do I have of my value? And is there a real path to getting what I want, here or elsewhere?

Her key distinction is between leaving from a position of strength and leaving out of frustration. The first is strategy. The second is just reaction. Someone who leaves with documented wins, an active network and several options negotiates very differently from someone who quits after a bad week.

This connects to her wider message about leverage. On a recruiting podcast she summed it up as: salary is about leverage, not worth. And in her newsletter she makes the point that your manager controls a small annual merit increase, while the market controls your next big jump. Knowing your market value, even if you choose to stay, is part of being strategic.

## Signs of a career that has stalled

Fifth, a few of her related video themes help with the diagnosis. One is "The career that got you here won't get you there": the skills that made you successful at one level can hold you back at the next. Another is "Things high performers normalise that actually hurt their careers", like always saying yes, being the reliable one who never gets stretched, or never asking for more. If you recognise yourself, it may not be the company. It may be the pattern.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas warns against making career moves based on mood. Her scorecard rates your role on money, culture, development, sponsorship and connections, from one to three each, and the pattern of low scores shows where your risk is building. And before you decide, ask what leverage and evidence you have, because leaving from strength is strategy, and leaving from frustration is just reaction.
