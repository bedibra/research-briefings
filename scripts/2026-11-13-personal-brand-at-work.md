# Building your personal brand at work

You already have a personal brand at work. It's what people say about you when you're not in the room. It's the first name that comes to mind, or doesn't, when a leader needs someone for a tricky project. The only question is whether you shape that reputation deliberately, or leave it to chance. In this episode, we'll look at what a personal brand at work really is, how to define yours, and how to build it inside your organisation, without turning into a self-promotion machine.

## What a personal brand at work is

First, a definition. Forget social media influencers. Inside a company, your personal brand is your reputation: what you're known for, and how reliably you deliver it.

Dorie Clark, a Duke University business school lecturer and author of books including "Reinventing You" and "Stand Out", describes it in a practical way. Ask yourself: what are you known for? When do people come to you for advice, to lead a project, or to answer an important question? That's your current brand. In a 2026 interview, she framed personal branding at work not as social media, but as making your work visible: things like running a lunch-and-learn, asking sharp questions in meetings, speaking on a panel, or writing internally.

## Defining your "known for"

Second, defining the brand you want. Clark suggests looking for the overlap of three things: what you're good at, what you enjoy, and what the organisation needs. Where those three meet is the reputation worth building.

For a medical writer, that might be: "the person who can turn messy data into a clear regulatory story". Or "the go-to expert on oncology submissions". Or "the writer who keeps difficult reviews on track". Or "our team's expert on AI-assisted writing and quality control". Each is specific, valuable and memorable.

Clark gives the example of an engineer whose recognised expertise is collaborative problem-solving. That reputation, she says, became a huge asset, and his career moved quickly because of it. Notice it isn't a technical skill. Brands can be built on how you work, not only on what you know.

A useful exercise: write one sentence. "When people think of me, I want them to think: blank." Then ask three trusted colleagues what they think you're known for today. The gap between the two sentences is your work plan.

## Building the brand: consistency and proof

Third, how to build it. Brands are built on two things: consistency and proof.

Consistency means delivering your "known for" again and again, so the reputation becomes reliable. If you want to be known as the writer who keeps reviews on track, every review you run needs to be visibly well-organised. One great example isn't a brand. A pattern is.

Proof means creating visible evidence. Here are some ideas.

Teach. Run a session on your area of expertise. Teaching signals mastery faster than almost anything else.

Write internally. A short article on the intranet, a best-practice guide, or a template others use. Every time someone uses your template, your brand is reinforced.

Take the visible assignments. Volunteer for the projects that match the brand you want: the oncology submission, the AI pilot, the cross-functional process improvement.

Speak up in your area. In meetings, contribute where your expertise is relevant. Over time, people will start turning to you whenever that topic comes up.

Go external, selectively. Speaking at a conference, publishing in an association journal such as those of AMWA or EMWA, or sharing thoughtful posts on LinkedIn can strengthen your reputation inside the company too, because external recognition often carries weight internally.

## Relationships carry your brand

Fourth, remember that your brand lives in other people's minds, and travels through relationships. That connects to two earlier episodes in this series: visibility and sponsorship. The people who talk about you, especially senior ones, are the channels through which your brand spreads.

Clark also offers a useful caution about networking: be patient. She suggests not rushing to ask for favours from new contacts. Build the relationship first, show genuine interest, and offer value. When you eventually need support, it will come more naturally.

## Avoiding the pitfalls

Fifth, a few pitfalls.

Don't overclaim. A brand that doesn't match reality is quickly exposed. Build on genuine strengths.

Don't be everything. A brand that tries to cover every skill isn't memorable. Pick one or two things.

Watch your behaviour under pressure. Brands are often made or broken in difficult moments: how you handle a missed deadline, a heated review, or criticism. Being calm and constructive under pressure is part of your brand, even if you never say so.

And update it as you grow. The brand that got you to senior writer may not be the one that gets you to director. At more senior levels, the reputation shifts from "great at the work" to "great at leading the work and developing others".

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, as AI takes over more routine drafting, the human qualities in your brand become more valuable: judgement, strategic thinking, the ability to influence stakeholders, and trustworthiness. Clark's 2026 interview focused exactly on this: building a brand that stays relevant in an AI-disrupted job market.

## Recap

To recap. Your personal brand at work is your reputation, what people say about you when you're not in the room. Define it at the overlap of your strengths, your interests, and what the organisation needs, in one clear sentence. Build it through consistency and visible proof: teaching, writing, taking the right projects, and speaking up. Let relationships carry it. And update it as your career grows.
