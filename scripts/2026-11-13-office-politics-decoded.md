# Office politics decoded

"I don't do politics." Many good professionals say it with pride. But here's the problem: opting out of office politics doesn't mean politics stops. It just means other people make the decisions that affect your work and your career, without you. In this episode, we'll decode what office politics really is, what research says about political skill, the difference between healthy and toxic politics, and how to take part ethically.

## What office politics really is

First, a definition that takes the drama out of it. Office politics is simply how people get things done when resources are limited and interests differ. Every organisation has limited budgets, headcount, promotions and attention. Every team has its own priorities. So people form alliances, trade favours, influence decisions, and compete for resources. That's politics. It isn't inherently good or bad. It's how organisations actually work, as opposed to how the organisation chart says they work.

In a pharma company, you see it everywhere. Which programme gets prioritised in the portfolio review. Which function gets the final say on the key message of a submission. Who gets the high-profile project. Who gets the headcount when budgets are cut. All of these are political processes, even when they're dressed up as purely rational decisions.

## Political skill: what research says

Second, the research. Organisational psychologist Gerald Ferris and colleagues developed the concept of political skill. They defined it as the ability to understand others at work and use that understanding to influence them in ways that support personal and organisational goals. Political skill has four dimensions.

One: social astuteness. Reading people and situations accurately. Noticing who really holds influence, what people care about, and the mood in the room.

Two: interpersonal influence. Adapting how you communicate to persuade different people, without being pushy.

Three: networking ability. Building a broad set of relationships across the organisation, and knowing who can help with what.

Four: apparent sincerity. Being seen as genuine, honest and trustworthy. This one is crucial. Influence attempts that feel manipulative backfire.

Here's why it matters. A large meta-analysis published in 2015 by Timothy Munyon, Gerald Ferris and colleagues found that political skill predicted job performance, even after accounting for personality and general intelligence. And the effect was stronger in jobs with more social and interpersonal demands, which describes most corporate roles. Part of the effect worked through reputation: politically skilled people built better reputations, which in turn improved how their performance was perceived.

In other words, political skill isn't a substitute for competence. It's what makes competence count.

## Healthy versus toxic politics

Third, the distinction that lets you engage with a clear conscience.

Toxic politics is about self-interest at others' expense: taking credit for others' work, spreading rumours, hoarding information, undermining colleagues, or manipulating people. It damages trust and, over time, usually damages the person doing it.

Healthy politics is about advancing good ideas and good work through relationships and influence: understanding stakeholders, building coalitions, framing proposals in terms others care about, and making sure the right people have the right information. Healthy politics serves both you and the organisation.

A simple test: would you be comfortable if everyone involved knew exactly what you were doing and why? If yes, it's probably healthy. If no, it probably isn't.

## How to play it ethically

Fourth, practical ways to engage.

Understand interests, not just positions. When the clinical lead insists on a particular message, ask what they're worried about. Perhaps it's a specific regulator question, or a commitment made to senior leadership. When you understand the interest, you can often find a solution that works for everyone.

Build relationships before you need them. The time to get to know the head of regulatory affairs is not the day you need their support on a contested decision. Have coffee, offer help, and show interest in their challenges long before.

Pre-wire important decisions. Before a big meeting, talk privately to the key people. Share your proposal, hear their concerns, and adjust. Decisions are often effectively made before the meeting, and the meeting just confirms them. Showing up cold to a big meeting with a surprising proposal is a classic political mistake.

Frame your ideas in terms of others' goals. Instead of "I want to change the template", say "This template change could cut a review cycle, which helps us hit the submission date leadership is focused on."

Stay out of gossip, but listen. Don't spread rumours, but do pay attention to the informal information flowing around you. It tells you a lot about priorities, tensions and upcoming changes.

Protect your reputation for sincerity. Keep your word. Be consistent. Tell the same story to everyone. In the long run, being trusted is the most powerful political asset you can have.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, politics often intensifies during reorganisations, mergers, leadership changes and budget cuts, all common in pharma. Those are the moments when your relationships and your understanding of the informal organisation matter most. In the next episode, we'll look at exactly that: how to map where power really sits.

## Recap

To recap. Office politics is simply how decisions get made when resources are limited and interests differ, and opting out just hands influence to others. Research shows political skill, built from social astuteness, influence, networking and sincerity, predicts performance. Practise healthy politics: understand interests, build relationships early, pre-wire decisions, and frame your ideas around others' goals. And protect your reputation for sincerity above everything else.
