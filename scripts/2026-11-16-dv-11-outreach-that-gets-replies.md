# Deepali Vyas: Outreach that gets replies

Deepali Vyas says she can tell within five seconds whether a networking message will get a reply. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at the second half of her hidden job market method: what to say once you've found the right person, and how to follow up without being annoying.

## Why most outreach is ignored

First, the five reasons she gives for messages being ignored.

It's about you, not them. Good outreach talks about the reader's problem, for example scaling a team after a funding round, not your career story.

It asks for a job. "Are you hiring?" forces a yes or no, and the easy answer is no. Ask for a conversation instead.

It's too long. Senior leaders read email between meetings, on their phones. If it doesn't fit on one phone screen, it's too long.

There's no proof. "Results-driven leader" means nothing. A specific number, like growing a team from four to forty people, does far more.

And it's one and done. According to Vyas, most replies come from follow-ups, not the first message.

## Four unwritten rules

Second, her rules. Silence isn't a no; it usually just means the person is busy. Timing beats talent; outreach works best right after news like a funding announcement or a new strategy. Give before you ask; share a relevant article, make an introduction, or offer congratulations. And make it easy to say yes. A specific ask, like twenty minutes sometime in the next two weeks, works better than a vague "let me know if you'd like to chat".

## The forwardable blurb

Third, the foundation of her method: a short, forwardable blurb about you. It has four parts. Who you are. The problem you solve. One proof point with a number. And what you're exploring. For example, a medical writing leader might describe herself as someone who builds regulatory writing teams for late-stage programmes, who cut review cycles by a third at her current company, and who is exploring head-of-function roles at growing biotechs.

The idea is that anyone can forward this blurb without rewriting it. You end every outreach message with it, and you send it to anyone willing to introduce you.

## Five kinds of message

Fourth, she describes five types of outreach. We'll paraphrase rather than quote her scripts.

One, congratulations to a hiring leader after news: congratulate them, connect what they're planning to your experience with one number, and ask for a short conversation.

Two, a note to a venture capital talent partner: mention specific companies in their portfolio, state your specialty, and offer to help, even by suggesting names.

Three, reconnecting with someone from your past: mention the shared history and something recent they've done, then ask for fifteen minutes.

Four, asking a mutual contact for an introduction, with your blurb attached so it's easy to forward.

And five, reaching out to a peer on the target team to learn what the work is really like.

## Follow-up and rhythm

Fifth, her follow-up schedule. Day one, send the email. Around day four, send a LinkedIn connection request. Around day ten, follow up with something useful, like an article or an idea relevant to them. Around day twenty-one, send a short final note. Then set a reminder for ninety days, and get back in touch when they announce news.

She recommends tracking everything in one simple spreadsheet: the company, what you think they need, who you contacted, when, and the next step. Review it every Friday. If you're not getting replies, change the message. If you're getting calls but they don't lead anywhere, change your pitch.

And she suggests a weekly rhythm of about thirty minutes a day. Monday, scan the news and update your target list. Tuesday, research a few companies and find the right person. Wednesday, send three to five direct messages. Thursday, network: reconnect with a few people, comment thoughtfully on posts from target companies, and make one introduction for someone else. Friday, follow up and update your tracker.

Crucially, she says to limit cold online applications to a handful a week, and only where you've also found a human contact. Measure conversations, not applications. Two or three real conversations a week is the goal.

## When you see the perfect posting

Finally, her advice for when a perfect job does appear online. Within twenty-four hours, apply on the company's own careers page. Find the hiring manager or a peer on the team and send a short note with one relevant fact and one number. And ask your network for a referral, with your blurb attached.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas says outreach fails when it's about you, asks for a job, runs too long, lacks proof, or never follows up. Write a short forwardable blurb with who you are, the problem you solve, one number and what you're exploring. Ask for a conversation, not a job, follow up on a schedule, and measure your search by conversations, not applications.
