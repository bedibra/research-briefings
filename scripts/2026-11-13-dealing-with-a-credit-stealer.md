# Dealing with a credit-stealer

It's one of the most frustrating moments at work. You spent weeks on an analysis, a document, or an idea. Then, in a meeting with senior leaders, a colleague presents it as their own, and everyone nods approvingly at them. In this episode, we'll look at how to handle a credit-stealer calmly and effectively: what to do in the moment, what to do afterwards, how to prevent it, and when to escalate.

## First, pause

The first step is the hardest: don't react in anger. Amy Gallo, a workplace conflict expert and contributing editor at Harvard Business Review, makes the point that we don't make rational choices when we're angry or upset. Publicly accusing a colleague in front of senior leaders usually backfires. It can make you look petty or insecure, even when you're completely right.

So take a breath. Your goal isn't revenge. It's two things: making sure the right people understand your contribution, and making sure it doesn't happen again.

## Consider the intent

Second, before deciding what happened, consider the possibilities. Sometimes credit-stealing is deliberate. But often it isn't. Your colleague may genuinely believe the work was collaborative. They may have said "I" out of habit when they meant "we". Your manager may present your work to their boss without naming you, simply because that's how they've always done it. In matrix organisations like pharma, where documents pass through many hands, the boundaries of who did what can genuinely be blurry.

Starting from the assumption that it may have been unintentional keeps the door open for a constructive conversation. If it turns out to be a pattern, you can change your approach.

## What to do in the moment

Third, if you're in the meeting when it happens, you can reclaim your contribution gracefully, without accusing anyone. A few approaches.

Build on it. "Thanks for raising that. I'd like to add some detail, since I developed that analysis. The key thing we found was..." You've made your role clear, and you've shown command of the material.

Ask a question that brings you in. "Happy to walk through how we got to that conclusion, if it's helpful."

Use "we" deliberately. "When I put together that framework last month, we found..." It's factual and calm.

The key is to sound collaborative and confident, not defensive. Senior people notice who really understands the work, especially when follow-up questions come.

## What to do afterwards

Fourth, after the meeting, talk to the colleague privately. Gallo recommends having a direct conversation with the person before going to your boss. Keep it factual and focused on the future. Something like: "In the meeting, the analysis came across as yours alone. I put a lot into it, and I'd like my part to be visible too. For the next update, can we agree on how we present it?"

Most people, when approached calmly, will adjust. Some will apologise. Either way, you've signalled that you notice, and that you'll speak up.

Then, follow up in writing where it's natural. A short email to the team or your manager: "Following today's meeting, attached is the full analysis I prepared, with the supporting data." It's not an accusation. It's simply documentation.

## Prevention: make credit hard to steal

Fifth, the best strategy is prevention. Make your contributions so visible that they're difficult to misattribute.

Agree on credit at the start. At the beginning of a joint project, agree who will present which parts to senior stakeholders. Gallo suggests exactly this: set expectations up front.

Put your name on your work. Document version history, author names, and short summaries of your contribution. In medical writing, keep clear records of who drafted and revised each section.

Send regular updates. Gallo suggests sending your manager regular updates on what you're working on. If your manager already knows you built the analysis, a colleague presenting it as theirs won't change their view.

Share ideas in public channels. Raise important ideas in meetings or in a group email, not just in a private chat with one colleague. Ideas shared publicly have a clear origin.

Build allies. Colleagues and senior people who know your work can speak up for you, sometimes in rooms you're not in. Gallo recommends finding someone, ideally with influence, who can speak up on your behalf.

## When to escalate

Sixth, escalation. If it keeps happening after a direct conversation, or if it's a big contribution with real career consequences, like a promotion case, then talk to your manager. Stay factual and focused on the business: "I want to make sure you have an accurate picture of my contributions to this project. Here's what I did." Bring evidence: drafts, emails, version history.

And if the credit-stealer is your manager, it's more delicate. Start by assuming they're presenting the team's work, which is part of their role. Ask for opportunities to present your work yourself: "Could I present the results at the next leadership meeting? It would be great development for me." A good manager will say yes. If they consistently refuse, a mentor or skip-level manager may be able to help.

## Be a credit-giver

Finally, the other side. Be generous with credit yourself. Name the people who helped you, specifically and publicly. It builds trust, it makes others more likely to credit you, and, as research on self-promotion shows, it makes your own claims more believable.

## Recap

To recap. Stay calm, and consider that it may not be deliberate. In the moment, reclaim your role by building on the point with confidence. Afterwards, talk privately and agree how credit works in future. Prevent it by agreeing on credit early, documenting your work, and sending regular updates. And escalate only with evidence, if it becomes a pattern.
