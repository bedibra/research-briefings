# Deepali Vyas: Sponsors and the hidden org chart

Your company has two organisation charts. One is on the intranet. The other is never written down, and it's the one that decides your career. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at what the executive recruiter says about mentors versus sponsors, the secret org chart, and why promotions are, in her words, political.

## Mentors give advice, sponsors create opportunities

First, the distinction Vyas returns to again and again. A mentor offers advice. A sponsor creates opportunities. Both are valuable, but they're not the same thing.

She paints a scene to make the point. A group of senior leaders is discussing who should fill a vice president role. Nobody is pulling up performance ratings. Then one leader says, "I know someone who'd be perfect for this." According to Vyas, that sentence, spoken in a room you're not in, is what actually moves careers.

So her rule is that anyone serious about advancing needs at least one sponsor: someone with real influence who will say your name when you're absent. But you can't simply ask someone to be your sponsor. That feels transactional. Instead, you earn sponsorship. You make your work visible to the right people, you deliver results they care about, and you prove you won't embarrass them if they put their reputation behind you. As she puts it in one newsletter, sponsors back people they see winning, not people who keep their heads down.

## The secret org chart

Second, what Vyas calls the unofficial org chart, or the influence map. It's the informal network that shapes decisions more than the official hierarchy does. One of her YouTube videos is titled simply "Your company has a secret org chart".

The key people on this map are often not the most senior. They're the people whose opinion others seek before deciding. The ones who give leaders a candid read on how things really are. The connectors who sit between different power centres. An executive assistant, a long-tenured programme manager, a respected scientist, a finance partner everyone checks with. When these people speak well of you, it travels.

Her practical exercise takes about twenty minutes. Look at who leaders defer to in meetings. Notice who gets copied on the important emails. See who runs the cross-functional forums. Then make sure those people know you and your work.

## Your boss isn't the only one deciding your future

Third, a theme from her recent video, "Your boss isn't the only person deciding your future". Vyas describes the calibration or talent-review meeting, where promotions are really decided. You aren't invited. Your manager has to argue your case in front of peers, often in a few minutes. If they lack conviction, or can't put your impact into words, the group simply moves on.

This leads to her recommendation to make your manager an advocate. Brief them before important meetings. Share wins proactively, not just at review time. And give them language they can reuse about you. Candidates who get promoted, she says, tend to have managers who arrive prepared and confident, because someone made it easy for them.

She also suggests a conversation roughly ninety days before the promotion cycle. Ask your manager what "ready for the next level" looks like here, and what evidence you should build between now and then. That single question signals ambition, clarifies the criteria, and turns your manager from a judge into a partner.

## Politics without losing your soul

Fourth, Vyas's view of office politics. Many capable people treat politics as something dirty and try to stay above it. She disagrees. In her framing, politics is simply the human side of organisational decisions: relationships, loyalties, blind spots and trust. Ignoring it doesn't make you more principled. It makes you less effective.

On a recruiting podcast called 10x Recruiting, she summed up the idea as learning to play politics without losing your soul. In practice, that means building genuine relationships before you need them, being generous with credit and introductions, and understanding who influences whom, rather than manipulating anyone.

## The playbook

Finally, her five-step playbook, paraphrased.

One, audit your relationships. List the ten people who matter most to your career, and label each one as mentor, sponsor, or neither. If you have no sponsors, that's the gap to close.

Two, map the influence network, as we described.

Three, create visibility above your manager. Each quarter, find an appropriate way to present work or share a perspective with someone two levels up. Exposure, not aggression.

Four, make your manager your advocate.

And five, separate performance from readiness. Are you doing your current job well, or are you visibly operating at the next level?

## Recap

To recap. Mentors give advice, but sponsors create opportunities, and Vyas says you need at least one person who'll speak your name in rooms you're not in. Every company has a hidden org chart of influencers and connectors, and getting on their radar matters as much as pleasing your boss. And because your manager has to argue for you in calibration, your job is to make that case easy to make.
