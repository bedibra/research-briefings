# Deepali Vyas: The BRAC system for documenting your wins

When your manager walks into the room where promotions are decided, what will they actually say about you? If the answer is "she's great, really solid", you're in trouble. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her system for documenting your impact, which she calls BRAC, and her advice on promotion timing.

## Why documentation matters

First, the problem. Vyas argues that promotions are decided by people, and people advance those they know, trust and can picture in the next role. But memory is short and busy managers forget. Your best work from last spring has faded by the autumn. So if you want someone to argue for you, you need to hand them the evidence. As she puts it in one issue, your self-review is ammunition, not a reflection exercise.

## The BRAC file and the BRAC book

Second, her two tools. They sound similar, but they do different jobs.

The BRAC file is your testimonial archive. It's the external evidence that your work matters. Thank-you emails from clients. Messages from colleagues saying your document saved them a week. Positive notes from your manager. Vyas's advice is to screenshot them and keep them in one place, so you can pull them out at review time. This is your social proof: what other people think of you.

The BRAC book is different. It's a living record of your impact in measurable terms. BRAC is an acronym. B stands for biggest wins. R stands for results with numbers. A stands for accomplishments others witnessed. And C stands for contributions above your level.

Examples might be revenue influenced, costs reduced, timelines shortened, projects shipped, or problems solved, ideally with a number attached. In a pharma or medical writing setting, that could be a submission delivered two weeks early, a review cycle cut from five rounds to three, or a template that saved every writer on the team a day per document.

Vyas suggests adding two or three entries every week. It takes minutes. And when review season comes, you're not trying to remember, you're choosing from evidence. Her summary: the file shows what others think of you, and the book shows what you actually delivered. You need both.

## The formula: what I did, what changed, what it was worth

Third, how to write each entry. Across several issues, Vyas uses the same three-part formula. What I did. What changed. What it was worth.

Compare two versions. "I launched the new client onboarding process." That's a task. Now the rewrite she offers: the new onboarding process cut time to revenue from forty-five days to twenty, and brought around two million dollars of first-year billings forward. That's a business case.

If you don't have an exact figure, she says, estimate responsibly and show your logic. A defensible estimate beats an adjective every time.

## Timing: decisions are made earlier than you think

Fourth, timing. Vyas argues that promotion groundwork is laid in the spring, and many decisions are effectively made in October, when budget cycles open and headcount is set. Waiting until December to make your case is too late.

So she recommends two meetings. The first, early in the year: ask your manager for thirty minutes, walk through what you've delivered, connect it to where the team is going, and ask what would make the rest of the year a clear promotion case. Her key question is "What does success look like?", so you can work backwards from the answer.

The second, booked in advance for early autumn: review the goals you agreed, show what you've already achieved, and discuss what a promotion or raise would look like before year end. And bring your BRAC book.

## The weekly email and the near miss

Fifth, two smaller habits that support the system. One is the two-minute weekly update to your manager: what you did, what's next, and where you need help. It keeps your manager informed without them asking, and it quietly builds your record.

The other is how to handle a "no". Drawing on a Wall Street Journal piece about turning a missed promotion into an opportunity, Vyas suggests using the near miss. A company that just passed you over may still see your potential. Ask for something concrete right away: a high-impact project, a mentor, coaching, or a clear plan for next cycle. Her tip is to ask immediately, because people find it hard to say no twice in a row.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas's BRAC system has two parts: a file of testimonials that shows what others think of you, and a book of quantified wins that shows what you delivered. Write each win as what I did, what changed, and what it was worth, and update it weekly. And time your case early, because by the time year-end reviews arrive, many decisions have already been made.
