# Deepali Vyas: Managing agents is not managing people

Two people are promoted to manager on the same day. One has fifteen years of experience and secretly doubts she belongs. The other is twenty-four, has spent eighteen months directing AI agents, and assumes he already knows how to manage. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at what she calls "overconfidence syndrome", and her toolkit for leading humans in the age of AI.

## Imposter syndrome's opposite

First, her framing. We talk a lot about imposter syndrome, the feeling of being a fraud despite evidence of competence. Vyas argues there's a newer, opposite problem. Overconfidence syndrome. And unlike imposter syndrome, which is painful and easy to spot, overconfidence feels good. So the person who has it is often the last to know.

She compares them. Imposter syndrome tends to hit experienced, mid-career people. Overconfidence tends to hit early-career, AI-fluent professionals. The hidden strength of the first group is empathy and a realistic sense of risk. The hidden strength of the second is speed, delegation and systems thinking. The first group's blind spot is their own competence. The second group's blind spot is other people's inner lives. And who notices first? For imposter syndrome, the manager. For overconfidence, the team, long before the manager does.

## What agents don't teach you

Second, why directing AI agents isn't the same as leading people. Vyas acknowledges it builds real skills: writing clear briefs, setting sharp success criteria, reviewing output with discipline. But agents remove the hardest parts of management. They have no feelings, so blunt feedback costs nothing. They have no agendas, so they don't lobby or quietly disagree. They need no motivation and hold no grudges.

So overconfident managers rarely fail on output, she says. They fail on trust. And by the time they notice, their best people are interviewing elsewhere.

She backs this with a recent survey by Wharton's Human-AI research group, reported by Fortune. Among managers at large American companies, daily AI use rose sharply in a year, and a large majority said their focus had shifted from creating work to managing AI-generated work. One gap stood out: managers were far more likely than junior employees to believe staff had received enough AI training. Vyas reads that gap itself as a sign of overconfidence. Reviewing AI output, she says, feels like management. But it isn't.

## The toolkit for new managers of people

Third, her advice to anyone managing humans for the first time: treat it as a new discipline, not a continuation of managing agents, and assume you're a beginner in the parts that matter most. She groups her advice into three areas.

Emotional intelligence. Listen more than you talk. In one-to-ones, ask what's getting in the way, then wait through the silence. Give feedback differently from correcting a machine: describe what you saw, explain why it matters, and make clear you want the person to succeed. Praise in public, correct in private. Watch for quiet signals, like shorter messages, cameras off, and fewer ideas in meetings. As she puts it, agents fail loudly; people disengage quietly. And keep a simple note on each team member: their career goals, what drains their energy, and how they like to be recognised.

Politics. Spend your first sixty days mapping real influence before launching anything. Pre-wire big decisions through one-to-ones. Build peer relationships when nothing is at stake. And ask who loses something if your initiative succeeds.

Self-awareness. Collect upward feedback within ninety days, ideally anonymously. Find a mentor at least a decade ahead of you who has handled layoffs and conflict. And keep a little healthy doubt, because it keeps you curious.

## The toolkit for those who feel like frauds

Fourth, her advice for the opposite problem, imposter syndrome. Rather than abstract confidence, she recommends evidence. Keep a Friday log: three lines a week, a decision you made, a problem you prevented, and a person you helped grow. Reread it before reviews and difficult meetings. Judge yourself on a few team metrics you own, like delivery, retention or engagement. Delegate at least one task a week, because hoarding work caps your team and keeps you invisible. Use the vacation test: if your boss were away, would this be your call? If so, decide, and then inform upwards. Speak early in meetings, with one question, one data point or one risk. And recognise your edge: your concern for people and consequences is exactly what overconfident peers tend to lack.

## Advice for organisations

Fifth, her suggestion for companies: pair seasoned managers with AI-fluent newcomers in reverse mentoring. Each has what the other lacks. Her closing line captures it: managing agents can improve your delegation, but only managing people makes you a leader.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas describes overconfidence syndrome, a newer problem where AI-fluent managers assume directing agents has prepared them to lead people. Agents have no feelings, agendas or motivation, so the hardest parts of leadership, trust, feedback and politics, still have to be learned. And for those with the opposite problem, imposter syndrome, her cure is evidence: a weekly log of decisions, problems prevented and people developed.
