# Strategic thinking

"Be more strategic." It's one of the most common pieces of feedback in performance reviews, and one of the least helpful, because it rarely comes with a definition. In this episode, we'll turn strategic thinking into something concrete: a set of six skills you can practice, a simple framework for making strategic choices, and what it looks like day to day in pharma and medical writing.

## What strategic thinking actually is

First, a working definition. Strategic thinking is the habit of connecting what you do today to where things need to be tomorrow, and making deliberate choices about what matters most. Execution is about doing things right. Strategy is about deciding which things are worth doing.

One of the most useful models comes from Paul Schoemaker and colleagues at Wharton and the consulting firm Decision Strategies International. Drawing on work with more than twenty thousand executives, they described six skills that strategic leaders share, published in the Harvard Business Review in 2013. Let's go through them.

## The six skills

One: anticipate. Strategic thinkers scan for early signals of change, before the change is obvious. That means talking to people outside your bubble, reading beyond your own function, and asking "what's coming?" In medical writing in 2026, that could mean noticing early that structured protocols under the new ICH M11 guideline, and generative AI drafting tools, are going to change how your team works, and preparing before you're forced to.

Two: challenge. This means testing assumptions, yours and other people's. Why do we always write the document this way? Does this section actually help the reviewer? What if the template is the problem? Strategic people invite different points of view instead of shutting them down.

Three: interpret. Once you challenge things, you get messy and conflicting information. Interpreting means finding the pattern in it. In a review cycle with forty conflicting comments, the strategic move isn't to resolve them one by one. It's to see that half of them come from one unresolved question about the key message, and to fix that question first.

Four: decide. Strategic thinkers avoid jumping too early into simple yes-or-no choices. They lay out a few real options, weigh the trade-offs, and then commit, even with incomplete information.

Five: align. A strategy nobody supports goes nowhere. Alignment means understanding what each stakeholder cares about, building trust, and bringing people along, especially when they disagree.

Six: learn. Strategic thinkers treat outcomes, good and bad, as information. They run honest lessons-learned reviews, and they're willing to change course when the evidence says they were wrong.

Schoemaker's team also made a sobering point. Strength in one of these skills can't easily make up for weakness in another. A great anticipator who can't align people will see the future clearly and still fail to change anything.

## A framework for choices: where to play and how to win

Second, a framework for making strategic choices. In the book "Playing to Win", A. G. Lafley, the former chief executive of Procter and Gamble, and the strategy thinker Roger Martin describe strategy as a cascade of five linked choices. What's our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win there? What capabilities do we need? And what management systems support it? Each choice constrains the next.

The two central questions are "where to play" and "how to win", and Martin argues they can't be separated. Interestingly, in a 2025 article he wrote that across all the strategies he's reviewed, the "how to win" box is consistently the weakest, because it's the hardest to answer honestly.

You can apply this at a personal or team level. Imagine you lead a small medical writing group. Where will you play? Perhaps you decide to focus on late-phase regulatory submissions in oncology, and to outsource routine documents. How will you win? Perhaps by being the team that delivers submission-ready documents with the fewest review cycles. Then, what capabilities do you need, and what systems, such as templates, training and metrics, support that? Suddenly "be more strategic" becomes a set of concrete choices, including choices about what not to do.

## What it looks like in pharma and medical writing

Third, how this shows up in daily work in pharma.

Start with the end in mind. In drug development, the clearest example is the target product profile. The FDA has described it as a tool that embodies "beginning with the goal in mind": a summary of the drug's intended characteristics, written in the format of the label you hope to get. Strategic writers know the target product profile and the desired label claims, and they check whether each document builds the evidence for them. If a key claim in the label isn't supported anywhere in the clinical overview, that's a strategic gap, not just a writing issue.

Think across documents, not within them. A strategic writer asks: does the protocol set up the analyses we'll need in the study report? Will the study report support the summaries in Module 2? Is the benefit-risk story consistent from the investigator's brochure all the way to the submission? Many companies now use integrated evidence plans to coordinate what evidence is needed across clinical, regulatory, and market access functions, including health technology assessment. Knowing that plan lets you contribute at the level where decisions are made.

Ask "what decision is this supporting?" Every document serves a decision: a regulator approving a trial, an ethics committee, a payer, a physician. When you know the decision, you know what to emphasize and what to cut.

And manage your own time strategically. Not all tasks are equal. Protect time each week, even one hour, to step back: what's changing, what's at risk, and where could you add the most value? That small habit is where anticipation and learning actually happen.

## How to build the skill

Fourth, a few practical ways to build it. Read one thing a week outside your function: a regulatory update, an earnings call summary from your company, or a competitor's approval. Before each big task, write one sentence on the purpose and the decision it serves. In meetings, practice asking one "why" question and one "what if" question. And after each major deliverable, spend fifteen minutes on lessons learned. Over months, these small habits add up to the reputation of someone who thinks strategically.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, strategic thinking will matter even more for medical writers as AI takes over first drafts. The value moves upstream: to shaping the narrative, connecting evidence across documents, and advising teams on what the regulator needs to see. The writers who thrive will be the ones who can explain not just what a document says, but why it says it.

## Recap

To recap. Strategic thinking is six learnable skills: anticipate, challenge, interpret, decide, align, and learn. Use "where to play and how to win" to turn vague ambition into clear choices, including what not to do. And in pharma, begin with the end in mind: know the target label, think across documents, and always ask what decision your work supports.
