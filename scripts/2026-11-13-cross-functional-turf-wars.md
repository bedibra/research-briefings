# Cross-functional turf wars

Medical writing says it owns the document. Clinical says it owns the message. Regulatory says it owns the strategy. Statistics says nobody touches the numbers. And somewhere in the middle, a submission deadline is approaching. In this episode, we'll look at why turf wars happen between functions, what research says about managing them, and practical ways to turn rivalry into real collaboration.

## Why turf wars happen

First, the causes. Cross-functional conflict isn't usually caused by difficult people. It's built into the structure of large organisations.

Different functions have different goals and incentives. Clinical development is measured on trial timelines. Regulatory affairs on approvals and agency relationships. Medical writing on document quality and delivery. Commercial on market access and uptake. Each function is doing its job, but their priorities collide.

They also have different expertise and language. A statistician and a marketer may look at the same data and see completely different stories.

And there's often ambiguity about decision rights. Who has the final say on the wording of a key efficacy claim? If that's never been agreed, every document becomes a negotiation.

Finally, there's identity. People care about their function's reputation and influence. When one group feels its expertise is being ignored, conflict quickly becomes personal.

## Conflict isn't the enemy

Second, an important insight from research. In 2005, Jeff Weiss and Jonathan Hughes of Vantage Partners published an influential Harvard Business Review article titled "Want Collaboration? Accept, and Actively Manage, Conflict". Their argument was counterintuitive. Many companies try to fix collaboration problems with teamwork training, incentives or reorganisations. But these don't work on their own, because conflict between functions is inevitable. It comes from the differences that make cross-functional teams valuable in the first place. The real goal is not to eliminate conflict, but to manage it well.

They noted that conflict often arises in the fast, unstructured interactions between groups. And they proposed two types of strategies: managing disagreements at the point where they occur, and managing them well when they escalate up the management chain.

## A shared goal above the functions

Third, Patrick Lencioni's idea from his book "Silos, Politics and Turf Wars". He argues that silos break down when leaders give everyone a thematic goal: a single, shared, time-bound rallying cry that every function commits to for the next few months. For example: "Submit the marketing application by June with no major gaps." When everyone shares one overriding priority, it becomes harder to defend your own turf at the team's expense. Lencioni also asks a good question: why wait for a crisis to rally people together? Crises naturally unite teams, so leaders should create that shared sense of purpose deliberately.

## Practical tactics

Fourth, practical tactics you can use, whatever your level.

Clarify decision rights early. At the start of a project, agree who decides what. A simple framework like RACI, which stands for responsible, accountable, consulted and informed, can prevent months of conflict. For example: clinical is accountable for the scientific interpretation, medical writing for structure and clarity, regulatory for strategy and agency alignment, and statistics for the numbers. Write it down and share it.

Agree on a common method for resolving disagreements. Weiss and Hughes recommend agreeing on criteria for trade-offs before conflicts arise. What matters most: speed, regulatory risk, or scientific accuracy? When you have agreed criteria, disagreements become discussions about evidence, not power.

Separate the people from the problem. Focus on the interests behind each function's position. If regulatory insists on conservative wording, ask what risk they're worried about. If clinical wants a strong claim, ask what evidence they're relying on. Often there's a solution that addresses both.

Escalate together, not against each other. One of the most practical ideas from Weiss and Hughes: when two functions can't agree, they should escalate jointly. Both sides go to their managers together, present the options and trade-offs, and ask for a decision. That's very different from each side running to its boss to complain about the other. Joint escalation keeps trust intact.

Build relationships across functions before conflict strikes. Have lunch with your regulatory counterpart. Ask the clinical lead about their pressures. Small investments in understanding make big disagreements easier.

Use the document as a neutral space. In medical writing, a clear comment resolution log can depersonalise conflict. Every comment is recorded, the rationale for each decision is documented, and unresolved items are escalated with options. It turns a battle into a process.

## The medical writer as broker

Fifth, a specific opportunity for medical writers. Writers sit at the crossroads of nearly every function. That position, which often feels like being caught in the middle, is actually a source of influence. A writer who can facilitate a tense review, summarise competing views neutrally, and propose a compromise is doing real leadership work. In promotion discussions, this ability is often what distinguishes a principal writer from a senior one.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, structured content and new standards like the ICH M11 protocol template will force functions to agree on shared content earlier. That could reduce some turf wars, but it may also create new debates over who owns the shared content. Writers who help design those processes will shape how the new boundaries are drawn.

## Recap

To recap. Turf wars come from structure: different goals, expertise, and unclear decision rights. Research says conflict can't be eliminated, so manage it: agree on decision rights and trade-off criteria early, focus on interests, and escalate jointly, not against each other. Give everyone a shared rallying cry. And as a medical writer, use your position at the crossroads to become the broker who brings functions together.
