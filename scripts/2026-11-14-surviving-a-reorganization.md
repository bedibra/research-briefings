# Surviving a reorganisation

The announcement arrives in a calendar invite: "Organisational update, all staff." Within minutes, the rumours start. Whose team is merging? Which roles are moving to another site? Is my job safe? In pharma, where mergers, portfolio reviews and restructurings are routine, most people will live through several of these in their career. In this episode, we'll look at what research says about reorganisations, the early warning signs, and how to protect and even advance your career before, during and after.

## Why reorganisations are so common, and so messy

First, some context. Companies reorganise to cut costs, integrate an acquisition, refocus on new therapeutic areas, or adopt new technology. And they do it often. McKinsey has surveyed thousands of executives about organisational redesigns over the years. One striking finding: only a minority of redesigns fully meet their objectives and improve performance. In one McKinsey survey, fewer than a quarter of respondents said their reorganisation had done both. Many efforts stall during implementation.

Two problems came up repeatedly in those surveys. Employees were distracted and anxious, and leaders focused too much on the organisation chart, the boxes and lines, rather than on how work would actually change day to day.

That's useful to know. It means confusion during a reorganisation is normal. And it means people who bring clarity about how the work gets done become very valuable.

## The early warning signs

Second, how to see it coming. Reorganisations rarely come from nowhere. Watch for these signals.

A new senior leader arrives. New leaders often reshape their organisation within the first six to twelve months.

The company announces a merger, an acquisition, or a major cost-saving programme.

A strategic review of the pipeline or portfolio is announced, or programmes are cancelled.

Consultants appear and start interviewing people about roles and processes.

Hiring freezes, backfills that never get approved, and decisions that are suddenly delayed.

And your manager becomes unusually vague about the future.

None of these guarantees a restructuring. But together, they're a signal to prepare.

## Before: prepare

Third, what to do before anything is announced.

Make your value visible and clear. In a restructuring, decision-makers often have very little time and very little information. People whose contribution is well understood are easier to keep, and easier to place in the new structure. Keep your impact log up to date, and make sure your manager and other senior leaders know what you deliver.

Strengthen your network across the organisation. Relationships with leaders in other functions and sites become lifelines when structures change. Your sponsor, if you have one, matters most now.

Align with where the company is going. If the strategy is moving towards a particular therapeutic area, toward AI-enabled processes, or toward outsourcing, build skills and experience that fit the future, not the past.

And quietly keep your options open. Update your CV and LinkedIn profile, and stay in touch with your external network. That's not disloyalty. It's prudence, and having options reduces anxiety.

## During: stay steady and useful

Fourth, when the reorganisation is under way.

Keep performing. When everyone is distracted, steady performers stand out. Leaders notice who keeps delivering while others spend their days speculating.

Stay out of the rumour mill, but listen. Don't spread speculation. But do pay attention to what you hear, and verify it through trusted people.

Ask good questions. In meetings with leaders, ask constructive questions: "What will success look like for our team in the new structure?" "How will decision-making change?" These show engagement and help you understand the new priorities.

Help others through it. People who support colleagues, explain changes and keep the team focused are showing exactly the leadership that new structures need.

And if your role is affected, take time to understand your options, your rights and the support available. In many European countries, including France, restructurings involve formal consultation processes and legal protections, so it's worth understanding your situation and getting advice where needed.

## After: reposition

Fifth, after the dust settles. A reorganisation is also an opportunity.

Build a relationship with your new boss early. Ask about their goals, pressures, and how they like to work. That's managing up, in a moment when it matters most.

Map the new power structure. Who has gained influence? Who has moved? The informal networks change too, so update your power map.

Volunteer for the work of making the new structure function: new processes, handovers, templates, onboarding. Remember McKinsey's finding: reorganisations often fail at the level of how work gets done. People who fix that become highly visible.

## Recap

To recap. Reorganisations are frequent and often messy, and confusion is normal. Watch for early signs like a new leader, mergers, portfolio reviews and hiring freezes. Before, make your value visible, strengthen your network and keep options open. During, keep performing, avoid rumours and help others. And after, build a strong relationship with your new boss and help make the new structure actually work.
