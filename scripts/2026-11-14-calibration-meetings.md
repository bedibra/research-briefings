# Inside the calibration meeting

Every year, in many large companies, a group of managers sits in a room, or on a video call, with a spreadsheet of names. In a few hours, they decide who is rated outstanding, who is "meets expectations", and who is ready for promotion. You're not in that room. But what happens there can shape your pay and career for years. In this episode, we'll look inside the calibration meeting: how it works, where bias creeps in, and what you can do in the weeks before.

## What calibration is

First, the basics. Calibration is a process used by many large companies, including most big pharma organisations, to make performance ratings consistent across teams. Without it, one manager might rate everyone as excellent while another is very strict. So managers first propose ratings for their own people. Then they meet as a group, usually with a senior leader and someone from human resources, to compare, challenge and adjust those ratings. Often, the same session discusses promotion candidates and high-potential employees.

The intention is fairness. Every manager has to justify their ratings in front of their peers.

## How it really works

Second, how it plays out in practice. A calibration discussion often moves quickly. Each person may get just a few minutes. Your manager presents a short case. Other managers add what they know of your work, or ask questions. Then the group agrees a rating, sometimes against a target distribution, meaning only a certain share of people can receive the top ratings.

This has a few important consequences.

Your manager is your advocate. Practitioners who study calibration consistently make the same observation: outcomes depend heavily on how well your manager makes your case. A manager who comes prepared with specific examples and clear impact will win more for their team than one who says "she's great, really solid".

Other people's impressions matter. When your manager speaks, other managers react based on what they've seen of you. If a director from another function has seen you lead a difficult review well, a single supportive comment can move your rating. If nobody else in the room knows your work, your manager is arguing alone.

Recent and visible work counts most. Human memory is short. Wins from eleven months ago fade. The project that went badly last month is vivid.

## Where bias creeps in

Third, the biases. Human resources specialists and researchers highlight several common ones.

Recency bias: the last few months outweigh the whole year.

The halo effect: one impressive trait or achievement colours everything else.

Similarity bias: managers tend to rate people who share their background, style or interests more highly, even when performance is equivalent.

And central tendency: ratings drift towards the middle, because extreme ratings require more debate. A respected HR publication, the Society for Human Resource Management, has even published commentary on how calibration meetings themselves can add bias, when the loudest or most senior voice dominates the discussion.

Good facilitators counter this by putting evidence first, before opinions, and by pushing back on vague justifications. But you can't control the facilitator. You can control the evidence.

## What you can do

Fourth, practical steps, mostly in the weeks and months before calibration.

Keep an impact log all year. Every few weeks, write down what you delivered, the result, and any feedback you received. A two-line entry is enough. "Led the clinical study report for the phase three trial. Submitted two weeks early. Clinical lead praised the clarity of the efficacy section." This beats trying to remember in December.

Give your manager the ammunition. A few weeks before ratings are proposed, send a short summary of your three to five biggest contributions, with outcomes and numbers. Frame it to make their job easy: "To help with year-end, here's a summary of my key results." Managers are busy, and many will use your words almost directly in the meeting.

Make sure others in the room know your work. This connects to earlier episodes on visibility and sponsorship. Present your results to senior stakeholders. Work on cross-functional projects. Build relationships with managers in neighbouring teams. The goal is that when your name comes up, other voices in the room can confirm your impact.

Ask your manager about the process. It's perfectly reasonable to ask: "How does calibration work here? What do you need from me to make a strong case?" And if you're aiming for promotion, ask what evidence the committee will look for.

Finish the year strongly. Because of recency bias, the last two or three months before calibration matter. That's not the time to coast.

And after the ratings, ask for specific feedback. If the result disappoints you, stay calm and ask: "What would have needed to be different for a higher rating? What should I focus on for next year?" Use the answer to plan the year ahead.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, some companies are moving away from single annual ratings towards continuous feedback, and others are using data and AI tools to flag inconsistencies in ratings. But wherever humans make comparative judgements, the same principle holds: decisions follow evidence and advocacy.

## Recap

To recap. In calibration, managers compare and adjust ratings as a group, often in minutes per person. Your manager's preparation and other people's impressions of you heavily influence the outcome, and biases like recency and similarity creep in. So keep an impact log, give your manager a clear summary before ratings are proposed, make your work known to other managers, and finish the year strongly.
