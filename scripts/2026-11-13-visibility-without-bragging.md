# Visibility without bragging

Many talented people share the same quiet belief: if I do excellent work, people will notice. It's a lovely idea. It's also one of the most common reasons good people get overlooked. In this episode, we'll look at why visibility matters, what research says about self-promotion that works and self-promotion that backfires, and a set of practical habits that make your work visible without making you that person who brags.

## Why good work isn't enough

First, the problem. Your manager is busy. Their manager is even busier. Decision-makers see a tiny slice of what you actually do, usually the slice that went wrong, or the slice someone talked about. Work that runs smoothly is, almost by definition, invisible. In medical writing, a study report that sails through review with no drama looks easy from the outside, even if it took exceptional skill to make it that way.

The career coach Harvey Coleman popularised a simple model for this, often called PIE: performance, image and exposure. Performance is the quality of your work. Image is what people think of you. Exposure is who knows you and your work. His point was that performance gets you in the game, but image and exposure drive promotions. The percentages people quote for his model vary, and it's a rule of thumb rather than a scientific finding. But the core idea rings true for most people who've sat in a promotion discussion: the people deciding can only advocate for work they know about.

## What research says about self-promotion

Second, the research, because there are good and bad ways to do this.

Start with what doesn't work: the humblebrag. Researchers Ovul Sezer, Francesca Gino and Michael Norton studied this in a paper published in 2018. A humblebrag is a brag disguised as a complaint or as modesty. "Ugh, I'm so exhausted from presenting to the leadership team again." Their studies found humblebragging was less effective than straightforward bragging. It reduced how much people liked the speaker, and how competent they seemed, because it felt insincere. In one field experiment, people were noticeably less willing to sign a petition for a humblebragger than for someone who simply stated their achievement. So the first lesson: if you're going to share a success, share it plainly.

Next, what does work. Jeffrey Pfeffer at Stanford and colleagues ran studies, published in 2006, on what they called the self-promotion dilemma. When you praise yourself, people discount it. But when someone else says the same thing about you, it's far more persuasive, even when everyone knows that person is on your side. The practical implication is powerful: build relationships with people who'll speak about your work, and do the same for them.

And a related finding from Maurice Schweitzer at Wharton and colleagues: "dual promotion". When people shared their own achievement and also credited someone else, they came across as both more competent and more likeable than people who only promoted themselves, even with identical accomplishments. Generosity makes your own claims more credible.

## Practical habits for visibility

Third, the practical part. Here are six habits that create visibility without bragging.

Habit one: frame updates around outcomes, not effort. Nobody wants to hear how hard you worked. They want to know what changed. Instead of "I've been working late on the clinical overview", try "The clinical overview is ready for review, two days ahead of plan, and I've flagged the three messages that need a decision from the team."

Habit two: send a short, regular update. A brief weekly or fortnightly email to your manager: what you delivered, what's coming, and where you need help. It takes ten minutes, it makes your manager's life easier, and it creates a written record of your contribution for performance review time.

Habit three: teach what you know. Run a short lunch-and-learn on something you've mastered: a new template, the ICH M11 protocol guideline, or a smart way to use your company's AI tools. Teaching makes you visible as an expert, without a single sentence of self-praise.

Habit four: speak once in every important meeting. Not to fill airtime, but to add something: a sharp question, a summary of where the group has landed, or a clear recommendation. People remember who moved the discussion forward.

Habit five: give credit loudly. Publicly thank colleagues who helped, and be specific. This is dual promotion in action. It also builds the reciprocal relationships that lead others to talk about your work.

Habit six: ask for the visible pieces of work. Volunteer to present the results to senior stakeholders, to represent your function in a cross-functional meeting, or to lead the lessons-learned review. Visibility often comes down to who's in the room.

## Handling the discomfort

Fourth, the discomfort. Many people, especially those raised to believe modesty is a virtue, feel that any self-promotion is distasteful. A useful reframe: making your work visible isn't about you, it's about information. Your manager needs accurate information to allocate projects, defend budgets, and promote the right people. If you hide your contribution, you're giving them incomplete data. Visibility is a service, not vanity.

It also helps to separate facts from adjectives. "I'm an amazing writer" is bragging. "The regulator accepted the submission with no major questions on the clinical sections I wrote" is a fact. Facts let people draw their own conclusions, and conclusions people draw for themselves stick.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, visibility is becoming harder in hybrid and remote work, where there are fewer chance conversations and less "being seen" in the corridor. That makes deliberate habits, like written updates and speaking up in virtual meetings, even more important.

## Recap

To recap. Good work is necessary, but invisible work rarely gets rewarded. Avoid humblebragging: state achievements plainly, framed as outcomes. Let others speak for you, and credit others generously, because both make your claims more believable. And build simple habits: regular updates, teaching, speaking once in every key meeting, and volunteering for the visible work.
