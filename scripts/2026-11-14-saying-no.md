# Saying no without hurting your career

Every yes you give at work is also a no to something else: to your most important project, to your thinking time, or to your evenings. Yet many capable professionals find it almost impossible to say no, especially to senior people. In this episode, we'll look at what research says about overload at work, why saying no feels so risky, and practical ways to decline, renegotiate and prioritise, in ways that actually strengthen your reputation.

## The overload problem

First, the scale of the problem. Rob Cross, a professor at Babson College, has spent years studying collaboration in organisations. In a widely cited Harvard Business Review article in 2016, written with Reb Rebele and Adam Grant, he reported that the time managers and employees spend in collaborative activities, meetings, calls and emails, had grown by fifty percent or more over the previous two decades. And the load isn't evenly shared. A small group of highly helpful, highly knowledgeable people tends to receive a disproportionate share of requests. Those are often exactly the people companies most want to keep, and they're the ones most at risk of burnout.

The good news: in later research, Cross and colleagues found that people who took just four or five practical steps to manage their collaboration recovered roughly eighteen to twenty-four percent of their collaborative time. That's close to a full day a week.

## Why saying no is hard

Second, why it feels so difficult. Cross's research points to a surprising cause: roughly half of collaboration overload comes from our own beliefs and motivations. We say yes because we want to be seen as helpful, knowledgeable or indispensable. We fear missing out. We worry about letting people down, or about losing control if someone else does the work.

Those are understandable motives. But they mean that the first step to saying no is honest reflection. Why am I saying yes to this? Does it serve my priorities, or only my wish to be liked?

There's also a career myth: that the person who always says yes gets promoted. In reality, people are promoted for impact on the things that matter, not for volume. The person who takes on everything often delivers nothing exceptional.

## Know your priorities first

Third, the foundation. You can only say no well if you know what you're saying yes to. Agree your top priorities with your manager, ideally explicitly: "These are the three deliverables that matter most this quarter." When a new request comes in, you can then evaluate it against those priorities, rather than against your guilt.

Some people keep a simple set of criteria. Does this request support one of my key objectives? Is it something only I can do? Does it build a skill or relationship I need? Will it make a visible difference? If the answer to all of these is no, it's a strong candidate for a polite decline.

## How to say no well

Fourth, practical techniques. Saying no is mostly a communication skill.

Say no to the task, not the person. Show you value the request and the relationship. "I really appreciate you thinking of me for this."

Explain briefly, with your priorities. "I'm fully committed to the submission documents until the end of the month, and taking this on would put that at risk."

Offer an alternative where you can. "I can't take on the full review, but I could look at the efficacy section." Or: "I can't do it this week, but I could in two weeks." Or: "My colleague has deep experience with this, would it help if I introduced you?" This last option can also create opportunities for others.

Ask for help prioritising. This is the most powerful technique with your manager or a senior stakeholder. Instead of saying no, say: "I'm happy to take this on. Which of my current priorities should I deprioritise to make room?" It's not a refusal. It's a trade-off conversation, and it shows strategic thinking. Often, the person realises the new request is less important than what you're already doing.

Don't over-apologise or over-explain. A short, clear, friendly answer is more professional than a long justification.

And buy time when you need it. You don't have to answer immediately. "Let me look at my commitments and come back to you tomorrow" gives you space to decide properly.

## Saying no to senior people

Fifth, the hardest case: senior leaders. The principles are the same, but framing matters even more. Connect your answer to their goals. "I want to make sure the June submission stays on track, since I know that's critical for you. If I take on this analysis, the timeline for the clinical overview slips by a week. How would you like me to balance them?" You're not refusing. You're helping them make an informed decision. Most senior leaders respect people who protect the organisation's priorities.

## Protect your time systematically

Finally, a few habits that reduce the number of times you need to say no. Block time in your calendar for focused work. Decline meetings where your presence isn't needed, or ask for the summary instead. And notice recurring requests that you could solve once, with a template or a training session, instead of answering again and again.

## Recap

To recap. Collaboration overload is real, and it falls hardest on the most helpful people. Roughly half of it comes from our own reasons for saying yes, so reflect honestly. Know your priorities, and judge requests against them. Say no to the task, not the person, offer alternatives, and with senior people, turn a refusal into a trade-off conversation. Saying no well isn't a career risk. It's a sign that you think strategically.
