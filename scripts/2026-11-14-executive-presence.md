# Executive presence

"She has great presence." "He's not quite ready, he lacks gravitas." Phrases like these are often decisive in promotion discussions for senior roles, yet they're rarely explained. In this episode, we'll demystify executive presence: what research says it's made of, why it matters, and concrete ways to build it, especially in high-stakes meetings with senior leaders.

## What executive presence is

First, a definition. Executive presence is the impression that you're a leader, that you can be trusted with important decisions, high-stakes situations and senior stakeholders. It's the quality that makes people listen when you speak.

The economist Sylvia Ann Hewlett and her Center for Talent Innovation studied it systematically. In 2012, they surveyed 268 senior executives, at director level and above, about what executive presence really consists of. They found three components, and the executives weighed them very differently.

Gravitas was by far the most important. Around two thirds of the executives, sixty-seven percent, said it was the core of executive presence.

Communication came second, at about twenty-eight percent.

And appearance came last, at about five percent. Hewlett noted that appearance mostly works as a filter: it can count against you if it distracts, but it rarely makes you a leader on its own.

Hewlett ran a follow-up survey in 2022, and wrote that while the overall picture held, appearance was the element whose meaning had changed the most, with far more emphasis on authenticity and less on formal dress codes in a hybrid world.

## Gravitas

Second, gravitas, the heart of it. In Hewlett's research, gravitas included things like confidence and grace under pressure, decisiveness, integrity, emotional intelligence, and a clear vision.

What does this look like in practice?

Grace under pressure. When a senior leader challenges your data in a meeting, do you get defensive, or do you stay calm, acknowledge the question and respond with substance? Composure in difficult moments is often what people remember.

Decisiveness. Leaders form a view and state it. "Based on the data, I recommend we submit with the current analysis, and address the subgroup question in the response to the agency." Even when uncertainty exists, a clear recommendation, with stated assumptions, signals leadership.

Knowing your stuff deeply. Gravitas rests on substance. You can't fake mastery of your material for long, especially with experienced scientific and regulatory leaders.

And integrity. Being willing to say "the data doesn't support that claim", even to a senior person, builds a deep kind of respect.

## Communication

Third, communication, the most coachable part.

Lead with the conclusion. Senior people are short on time. Start with your bottom line, then the reasoning. "We're on track for the June submission, with one risk I need your help on." Then the details.

Be concise. Prepare a version of your message that takes one minute. If they want more, they'll ask.

Speak with calm confidence. Avoid filler phrases like "I just wanted to quickly mention" or "this might be a silly question". Lower your speed. Pause. Silence after a key point gives it weight.

Command the room, including on video. On calls, turn your camera on, look at the camera when making key points, and be the person who summarises the discussion: "So, we've agreed on three things..." Summarising is a subtle but powerful leadership signal.

Handle tough questions well. If you don't know the answer, say so with confidence: "I don't have that number, I'll get it to you by end of day." That's far better than guessing.

## Appearance

Fourth, appearance, briefly. In today's corporate world, it's less about formal clothes and more about looking appropriate for the context, being well prepared, and avoiding distractions. On video, it includes good lighting, a clean background and reliable audio. Small things, but they shape first impressions.

## How to build it

Fifth, how to develop executive presence.

Ask for specific feedback. Executive presence is about how others perceive you, so you need their input. Ask a mentor or sponsor directly: "What would make me come across as more senior in leadership meetings?" Vague feedback like "be more strategic" or "more presence" is common. Ask for specific examples.

Practise in lower-stakes settings. Present at team meetings, run a lunch-and-learn, or chair a cross-functional review. Each time, focus on one skill: leading with the conclusion, pausing, or handling questions.

Prepare for high-stakes moments. Before a senior meeting, prepare your key message, your one-minute version, and answers to the three toughest questions you could be asked.

Watch people you admire. Notice how respected leaders open a presentation, handle disagreement or deliver bad news. Borrow what fits you.

And stay authentic. Executive presence isn't about becoming someone else. The most credible leaders are recognisably themselves, just at their most composed, clear and decisive.

## Recap

To recap. Hewlett's research found that executive presence is mostly gravitas, then communication, and only a little appearance. Build gravitas through composure under pressure, decisiveness, deep knowledge and integrity. Communicate by leading with the conclusion, being concise and speaking with calm confidence. And develop it deliberately: seek specific feedback, practise in lower-stakes settings and prepare thoroughly for the big moments.
