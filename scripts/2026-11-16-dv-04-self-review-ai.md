# Deepali Vyas: Writing a self-review that survives AI

Here's a question few people ask during review season: who, or what, is actually reading your self-review? In her November newsletter, Deepali Vyas argued that, more and more, the first reader is an AI tool. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at what that means and how she suggests writing a review that survives.

## Most self-reviews disappear

First, her starting point. Based on twenty-five years in executive search, Vyas says most self-reviews are skimmed and forgotten. A manager skims the document. Increasingly, an AI tool condenses it into a few lines. And when salaries and promotions are actually discussed, nobody quotes it. What survives that process, she says, is visibility and proof points: wins someone can say out loud, defend when challenged, and support with a number.

## The data behind it

Second, the numbers she cites. They come from a Nielsen survey of around seventeen hundred directors and senior managers, commissioned by the software company Monday dot com and reported by TechCrunch. As Vyas summarises it, around four in ten leaders already use AI to help draft performance reviews. More than half of senior leaders say evaluating work has become harder since AI arrived, because polished output hides who actually did what. And nearly eight in ten say they've changed how they evaluate people, giving more weight to skills like delegating to AI and experimenting with it. These are survey findings, reported second hand, so treat the exact figures with some caution. But the direction is clear.

## Two readers

Third, her central framework. Your self-review now has two readers with different needs. The AI reader rewards specifics and drops adjectives. The human reader needs a story they can repeat in the calibration meeting. In her words, the AI needs evidence, and the human needs a reason. Most reviews give neither. They're lists of activities wrapped in adjectives.

For the AI reader, she offers four rules. Lead each win with a number. "Improved onboarding" disappears in a summary. "Cut onboarding from forty-five days to twenty" survives. Use one sentence per win, in her three-part formula: what I did, what changed, what it was worth. Three to five wins is enough. Name the people and teams involved, so the work is traceable. And delete the self-praise adjectives: passionate, dedicated, team player. If a machine would flag a sentence as generic, cut it.

For the human reader, she offers four more. Open with a single sentence summarising your year, written so your manager can repeat it word for word in the room. Add the context AI can't see: a reorganisation, a colleague you covered for, a project that failed for reasons outside your control. Keep it factual, not a complaint. Describe how you work with AI: one task you delegated, the time it saved, and how you checked the output. She frames this as a signal of judgment, which managers now reward. And end with one sentence about the bigger problem you want to own next year. That sets up the conversations about scope, promotion and pay.

## Using AI on your own review

Fourth, her rules for using AI to help write the review. Use it to edit, never to invent. Anything inflated will be caught in calibration and damage your credibility. Read the draft aloud. If it doesn't sound like you, your manager will notice. Plain and specific beats polished and generic. And keep a copy outside company systems, because it's raw material for future CVs, negotiations and interviews.

She also suggests a useful prompt. Paste in your draft and ask the AI to play a sceptical manager with a dozen reviews to get through. Ask it to flag every vague sentence and rewrite each win in the three-part format, adding nothing you didn't write.

## Advice for managers

Fifth, she has advice for managers too. Start from your own notes, not a blank prompt, so the AI organises what you observed rather than guessing. Never paste names or performance details into public AI tools. Use approved company tools. Rewrite any sentence that could apply to anyone; if you can swap the name without changing the meaning, it isn't real feedback. Add one observation only you would know. And read your employee's self-review in full before writing yours, because people can tell when it's been skimmed.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that your self-review now has two readers: an AI that rewards numbers and specifics, and a human who needs a story to repeat. Lead every win with a number, cut the adjectives, add context only you can provide, and mention how you use AI with good judgment. And use AI to edit your review, never to invent it.
