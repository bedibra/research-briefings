# Negotiating salary and promotion

Most people don't lose money in a negotiation because they negotiate badly. They lose it because they never really negotiate at all. They accept the first number, or they ask once, hear "that's our standard band", and stop. In this episode, we'll look at what negotiation research actually shows, how to negotiate a promotion inside your current company, how to handle a new offer in pharma, and what Europe's new pay transparency rules change for you.

## The two ideas that matter most

First, the two concepts that sit under almost every piece of good negotiation advice.

The first is your alternative. Negotiation researchers call it your BATNA, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. In plain terms: what will you do if this conversation fails? Stay in your role another year? Accept an offer from another company? Move to a contract research organization? Your alternative is your real source of power. A 2015 study by Michael Schaerer, Roderick Swaab and Adam Galinsky found that negotiators with a strong alternative set higher goals, made more ambitious first offers, and claimed more value. But there's a twist. When people had a clear but weak alternative, that weak number acted like an anchor and pulled their own demands down. So the lesson is twofold: work to improve your alternative before you negotiate, and don't let a weak fallback set your target.

The second idea is the anchor. The first number put on the table tends to pull the final outcome towards it. Experiments by Galinsky and Thomas Mussweiler found that negotiators who made the first offer usually ended up with better results than those who waited. Galinsky's own advice is that first offers should be ambitious, but not absurd. There's an important nuance, though. Research by Joe Magee, Galinsky and Deborah Gruenfeld suggests that if you're in a weak position, and the other side has a strong alternative, it can be wiser to let them speak first. So anchor when you have good information and a reasonable position. When you don't know the range, get information first.

## Negotiating a promotion internally

Second, negotiating inside your own company, which is different from negotiating a new job.

Here's the key insight about timing. By the time you're in the meeting where your manager tells you about a promotion, the decision, and often the number, has usually already been made. One adviser put it neatly: if you wait for that meeting, you're negotiating against a decision that's already been taken. The real window is the weeks before, when your manager is building the case and arguing for you with HR and senior leadership. So start the conversation early. Ask: "What would I need to show to be promoted in the next cycle?" Agree on two or three concrete goals, and check in every quarter.

Then make your manager's job easy. Your manager is your advocate in rooms you're not in. Give them a short written summary of your impact, with numbers. For a medical writer, that might be: "I led the clinical study report for the pivotal trial, coordinated eleven reviewers, and we submitted two weeks early." Or: "I trained three new writers and built the template the team now uses." Evidence beats adjectives every time.

Next, know the numbers. Career sources commonly describe promotion increases of around ten percent, sometimes up to twenty, but companies don't always offer the top of the range upfront. Look at the band for the new grade, not just the percentage increase on your current salary. If you're promoted but land at the very bottom of the new band, that's worth raising: "Given my experience, I'd expect to sit closer to the midpoint of the band."

And remember that internal negotiations often move more slowly, and usually produce smaller jumps than a new job offer. That's not a reason to leave, but it's a reason to know your market value. A real external alternative is the strongest card you can hold, as long as you only play it if you're genuinely prepared to go.

## Negotiating a new offer in pharma

Third, negotiating an offer from a new company. In pharma and biotech, the salary is only one part of the package. Think in terms of total compensation: base salary, annual bonus target, long-term incentives such as shares or restricted stock units, a sign-on bonus, relocation support, flexibility, and training budget.

At large pharma companies, bonus targets and equity grants are often fixed by grade, so the room to negotiate may be bigger elsewhere. A sign-on bonus is a classic lever, especially to replace a bonus you'd lose by leaving your current employer mid-year. If you're giving up an annual bonus, say so clearly and ask for it to be covered. Do check the clawback terms, though, meaning whether you'd have to repay the sign-on if you leave within a year or two.

Another lever is the grade itself. Getting the title right at entry, for example principal instead of senior, often matters more over the years than a few thousand on the base salary, because the grade sets your band, your bonus target, and your next promotion.

A few practical phrases help here. Instead of stating a number too early, ask: "What's the total compensation range for this role?" When you get an offer, thank them, show enthusiasm, and then say: "I'm very excited about this. Is there flexibility on the base, or on a sign-on to cover the bonus I'd be leaving behind?" Then stop talking. Silence is uncomfortable, but it's useful.

## What pay transparency changes in Europe

Fourth, a change that's especially relevant if you work in Europe. The EU Pay Transparency Directive had to be written into national law by the seventh of June 2026. It requires employers to tell job applicants the starting pay or pay range before the interview, ideally in the job ad, and it stops employers from asking about your salary history. Employees will also gain the right to ask about average pay levels for people doing the same work, broken down by gender.

In practice, many countries missed the deadline. A Deloitte update from July 2026 said only four member states had fully transposed it on time: Italy, Lithuania, Malta and Slovakia. France, for example, is expected to adopt its law later, possibly in 2027. But the direction is clear. Pay ranges are becoming public, and "competitive salary" will no longer be enough. For you, that means better information. Use the published range to anchor your ask, and if you're asked about your current salary, it's increasingly reasonable to say: "I'd rather focus on the value of this role and its range."

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, watch how your country implements pay transparency, because national rules will differ in the details. Watch how companies publish their pay bands, which will make internal comparisons much easier. And watch your own alternatives, because a strong alternative is the best negotiation preparation there is.

## Recap

To recap. Your power comes from your alternative, so improve it before you negotiate, and don't let a weak fallback anchor you low. For promotions, negotiate early, while your manager is still building the case, and give them evidence. For new offers, think total compensation, and use the sign-on and the grade as levers. And in Europe, pay transparency is putting real numbers in your hands, so use them.
