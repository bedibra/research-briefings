# Deepali Vyas: How layoff lists really get made

Most January layoffs, according to Deepali Vyas, are decided in October and November. By the time the rumours start, the list is usually written. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at how she says layoff decisions are made, the red flags to watch, and her toolkit for making yourself hard to cut.

## The five stages

First, her description of how the list gets made, in five stages.

Stage one, around September and October: the number comes down. Finance sets a target, perhaps holding costs flat or cutting operating expenses by a percentage. Headcount is usually the largest line.

Stage two, in October: scenarios. Leaders model a five percent cut, a ten percent cut, or hitting plan. At this point, they're discussing roles and teams, not people.

Stage three, in November: names. The boxes become people, in meetings between a leader, their boss and human resources.

Stage four, through November and December: review. Legal and HR check the list for risk, finance models severance, and timing is set around year-end close and bonuses.

And stage five, in January: the announcement. And everyone acts surprised.

Her point is that your window to influence the outcome is in stages one to three. Waiting for rumours means waiting too long.

## Six unwritten rules

Second, the rules she says govern these decisions.

Math comes before personal. Decision-makers ask what a role costs and what happens if it disappears, not whether you're a good person.

Roles get cut, not ratings. Strong performers can lose their jobs if their project is deprioritised.

Expensive plus invisible equals exposed. If your leader can't describe your impact in one sentence, you're an easy target.

Managers only defend what they can explain. They may get thirty seconds to argue for you.

Tenure isn't protection. Long-serving people can be the most expensive in their pay band.

And decisions are made by people who don't know you. Your reputation has to reach beyond one manager.

## Profitable companies cut too

Third, a theme from her video on Microsoft. She discussed reports that Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to tens of thousands of employees, despite strong revenue and profit. Her takeaway was that a successful company and a secure job are two different things. Companies don't manage headcount based on whether they're profitable, she says, but on whether leadership believes each dollar creates enough future value. Profitability doesn't protect a role. Strategic priority does. And being attached to a hot initiative, like AI, isn't the same as being essential to the business.

She suggests asking yourself an uncomfortable question. If your role disappeared tomorrow, what business problem would no longer be solved? Not your title or your meetings. What would break, slow down, become more expensive, or create risk?

## Red flags

Fourth, her warning signs. Your project loses budget, gets paused, or is folded into another team. You stop being invited to planning meetings. Your manager is vague about next year. Leaders start talking about efficiency, flattening, or doing more with less. A new leader is "assessing the organisation". Someone else is asked to document your work. Open roles on your team are frozen. Or your goals for next year are vague while your peers' are specific. She says three or more of these deserve your attention.

## The toolkit

Fifth, her five moves.

Move one: an alignment map. In thirty minutes, identify the two or three things leadership is betting on next year, from the earnings call, the all-hands deck, or by asking your manager. Then connect your work to them. If you can't, get attached to a priority.

Move two: the "hard to cut" test. Score yourself from one to five on revenue impact, risk if you left, key relationships, replacement cost, and visibility two levels up. Under fifteen out of twenty-five, she says, means you're more exposed than you think. Work on your two lowest scores first.

Move three: equip your manager. Before planning closes, send your top three wins in the format what I did, what changed, what it was worth.

Move four: a quiet plan B. Over ninety days, update your CV and LinkedIn, reconnect with around ten warm contacts, and aim for about six months of financial runway. Know your bonus timing, vesting dates and severance policy.

And move five: if the signals are loud, negotiate. Ask to move onto a priority project. Never sign a separation agreement on the spot. Severance, timing, references and how your departure is described are often negotiable. And if you suspect discrimination or retaliation, document everything and get proper legal advice. In France and much of Europe, collective redundancies follow formal consultation rules, so your rights may be stronger than in the United States, where most of her examples come from.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas says layoff decisions are made months before they're announced, so the time to act is during budget planning, not when rumours start. Roles are cut on math and strategic priority, and expensive plus invisible equals exposed. So align your work to next year's priorities, score yourself on the hard-to-cut test, arm your manager with your wins, and quietly build a plan B. As she puts it, hope is not a strategy, and neither is panic.
