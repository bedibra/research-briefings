# Deepali Vyas: The meeting before the meeting

You've built the perfect slide deck. The data is airtight. You walk into the meeting, present brilliantly, and nothing moves. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at what she calls one of the biggest executive secrets: the most important meeting often happens before the meeting.

## Pre-wiring the room

First, the idea itself, from one of her recent YouTube videos. Junior employees, Vyas says, walk into a conference room believing a decision will be made there. Senior executives often walk in already knowing where everyone stands, because they've pre-wired the room.

What does that look like? Before the meeting, they've called the chief financial officer. They've talked to the head of human resources. They've asked the chief operating officer what worries them. They've given the chief executive a heads-up. And they've dealt with objections privately, one on one, before anyone has to defend a position in public.

Is the decision completely predetermined? Not always, she says. But the groundwork has been laid. The meeting confirms, rather than debates.

## Why smart people get stuck

Second, why this frustrates so many capable professionals. Vyas describes people who work the presentation but not the stakeholders. They invest everything in the content, assuming the best argument wins. But at senior levels, decisions are made by people, with their own priorities, worries and relationships. A stakeholder who sees your proposal for the first time in a crowded room has every reason to be cautious. They might feel blindsided. They might not want to agree in front of their own boss before they've checked the impact on their team.

Her rule is simple and memorable: never let the big meeting be the first time a critical stakeholder hears your big idea. She calls that executive leadership one-oh-one.

## Four questions before any big decision

Third, how to do it. Before any major decision, Vyas suggests asking four questions. Who has veto power? Who has influence? Who's likely to object? And whose support will bring everyone else along?

In her newsletter on new managers, she adds a fifth question that's especially useful: who loses something if this initiative succeeds, and how can I bring them along? People rarely oppose ideas in the abstract. They oppose losing budget, headcount, status or control.

Then go and talk to those people. And here she's explicit: not to manipulate them, but to understand them. Ask what they need from the decision. Listen to their concerns. Adjust your proposal where it makes sense. Often a small change, like a different timeline, a pilot, or a role for their team, turns a likely opponent into a supporter.

## Applying it in a pharma setting

Fourth, let's translate this to a typical pharma environment. Imagine you want to change how clinical study reports are reviewed, for instance moving from several sequential review rounds to a single structured review meeting. The idea might save weeks. But it touches clinical, biostatistics, safety, regulatory and quality.

Pre-wiring would mean a short conversation with each functional lead before the governance meeting. You'd ask the statistics lead how the change affects their timelines, and the regulatory lead what risks they'd see. You'd ask quality what documentation they'd need. You'd incorporate their input, and maybe let one of them co-present. By the time the proposal reaches the committee, the key people have already shaped it. That's not politics in the negative sense. It's respect for their expertise, and it makes your idea far more likely to survive.

## Pre-wiring as a habit

Fifth, Vyas connects this to a broader habit: building relationships when nothing is at stake. In her advice to new managers, she says to spend the first sixty days mapping real influence, who people go to for advice and whose agreement actually matters, before launching anything. And to build peer relationships early, because your career runs on goodwill. Pre-wiring is much easier when you're calling someone you've already helped, not someone you only contact when you need something.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas argues that important decisions are often shaped before the meeting, through one-on-one conversations that address objections in private. Before any big proposal, ask who has veto power, who has influence, who will object, whose support matters most, and who stands to lose. Then talk to them to understand, not to manipulate, and never let a critical stakeholder hear your big idea for the first time in the room.
