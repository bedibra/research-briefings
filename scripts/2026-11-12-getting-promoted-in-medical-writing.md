# Getting promoted in pharma medical writing

Here is an uncomfortable truth about promotion in medical writing. The thing that got you hired, writing clean, accurate documents, is rarely the thing that gets you promoted. In this episode, we'll look at how the medical writing career ladder actually works inside pharma companies, what changes at each step, and the concrete moves that get you from one rung to the next, including how artificial intelligence is reshaping what "senior" means in 2026.

## The ladder

First, the map. At large pharmaceutical companies, the path usually runs from associate medical writer, to medical writer, to senior medical writer, then principal medical writer. After that, it splits. One branch leads into people management: medical writing manager, associate director, director, and eventually head of medical writing. The other branch stays technical, as an expert or lead writer for a whole therapeutic area or program. Some writers also move sideways into regulatory affairs, where knowing how documents fit together becomes submission strategy.

Job postings give a rough sense of the time involved. A recent Merck posting, for example, asked for roughly five years of experience with a bachelor's degree, or three years with a doctorate, for a senior role, and about ten years with a bachelor's, or five with a doctorate, for principal. Treat those as signposts, not rules. Every company sets its own bar, and moving between a contract research organization and a sponsor company often resets the title in either direction.

## What actually changes at each level

Second, and this is the core of it, what changes as you climb. A useful way to think about it is that each level widens your unit of responsibility.

At the junior levels, your unit is the section. You draft parts of a clinical study report or a protocol, you follow the template, and you learn the guidance documents, such as ICH E3 for study reports.

At the medical writer level, your unit is the document. You own a whole study report or investigator's brochure. You run the review cycle, you reconcile comments, and you hit the timeline.

At senior level, your unit becomes the project, and the skills shift from writing to influence. Job descriptions at this level keep repeating the same phrases: solving problems across the scope of work, negotiating with project team members, coordinating complex reviews with many stakeholders, and coaching junior writers or contractors. In practice, that means you're the person who tells a clinical lead, calmly and with a reason, that a key message isn't supported by the data. And you're the one who keeps a review on track when statisticians, clinicians, and regulatory colleagues all disagree.

At principal level, your unit is the program or the submission. You think across documents. Does the clinical overview in Module 2 of the common technical document tell the same story as the summaries and the study reports? Are the benefit-risk arguments consistent from the protocol all the way to the label? Principal writers are trusted to shape the regulatory narrative, not just record it.

And at manager and director level, your unit is the function: people, processes, resourcing, and vendors. A recent GSK posting described the manager as a change agent for continuous improvement across the global medical writing organization. That's the language of someone who improves the system, not just the output.

## How to get promoted: five practical moves

Third, the practical part. Here are five moves that consistently make the difference.

Move one: act at the next level before you have the title. Promotion committees promote people who are already doing the job. If you're a medical writer aiming for senior, volunteer to lead a full review cycle, run the kickoff meeting, or mentor a new joiner. Then keep a simple log of what you did and the result: a submission delivered early, a review cycle cut from three rounds to two, a junior writer who ramped up quickly.

Move two: build visible strategic judgment. The writers who stand out ask "what decision is this document supporting?" before they write a word. Learn the regulatory context behind your documents, for example what reviewers at the FDA or the European Medicines Agency will look for, and bring that perspective into team meetings. Speaking up with a well-reasoned recommendation is worth more than a perfect draft delivered in silence.

Move three: own a process or a standard. Senior and principal job descriptions often mention leading process improvements. Pick one: a better template, a style guide, a lessons-learned review, or a training session. There's a very timely option right now. The new ICH M11 guideline on a structured, harmonised clinical protocol was adopted internationally in November 2025. In Europe it took effect in June 2026, and the FDA finalized its version in May 2026. Most companies are still working out how to adopt it. Becoming your team's go-to person on M11 is exactly the kind of visible expertise that gets noticed.

Move four: become the person who uses AI well, and safely. Generative AI is now drafting first versions of documents from study data and templates at many companies. Industry voices, including Biogen's medical writing lead speaking at a RAPS conference, describe the writer's role shifting from producer to strategic owner. The writer still owns the document, checks every claim, and decides the narrative. A poster at ISPOR Europe 2026 framed it as moving from medical writer to medical editor. So learn the tools your company has approved, help build the quality checks around them, and position yourself as someone who makes the team faster without risking accuracy. That's a promotion story in itself.

Move five: invest in credentials and visibility. In the United States, the American Medical Writers Association offers the Medical Writer Certified credential, or MWC. It's an exam-based certification for people with at least two years of paid medical writing experience, and it has to be renewed every five years. In Europe, the European Medical Writers Association runs a professional development programme with workshop-based certificates. These recognise training, not competence, but they show commitment and build your network. If you work in publications, there's also the Certified Medical Publication Professional credential from ISMPP. Speaking at a conference or writing an article for an association journal raises your profile beyond your own team.

## Making the case

Fourth, how you actually ask. Don't wait for your annual review to discover the criteria. Ask your manager directly: "What would I need to show to be considered for senior or principal in the next cycle?" Then agree on two or three concrete goals and check in on them every quarter. Bring evidence, not adjectives. Instead of "I'm a strong writer," say "I led the study report for the pivotal trial, coordinated eleven reviewers, and we filed two weeks early."

Know your market value too. In the American Medical Writers Association's 2024 compensation survey, based on 2023 income, full-time medical communicators in pharma and biotech averaged around one hundred and sixty-six thousand dollars. That's well above writers in marketing and advertising. Salary sites show very different numbers for managers and directors, so use the association surveys as your main benchmark. Sometimes the fastest promotion is an external offer, and knowing your value helps in both conversations.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, there are three things to watch. First, how quickly your company adopts structured, data-driven documents like M11 protocols. Second, how AI quality-control roles get defined, and whether they sit inside medical writing. And third, whether companies create explicit technical career tracks, so that great writers can reach director-level pay without managing people.

## Recap

To recap. Each promotion in medical writing widens your unit of responsibility, from section, to document, to project, to program, to function. The fastest way up is to do the next level's job visibly: lead reviews, mentor others, show strategic judgment, and own a process. Right now, expertise in ICH M11 and in responsible use of AI are two of the clearest ways to stand out.
