# Deepali Vyas: Confident versus intimidating

You can be kind, warm and generous, and still be described as intimidating. You can also be so careful not to intimidate that nobody notices you at all. In this episode of our unofficial Deepali Vyas series, we'll look at her take on executive presence, and the fine line between confident and intimidating.

## Same behaviour, different feeling

First, her central observation. From the outside, confident and intimidating behaviour often look the same. The difference, Vyas says, is how the other person feels afterwards. Confident people leave others feeling seen. Intimidating people leave them feeling small.

She walks through common behaviours to show it. Silence is confident when you're visibly listening, and intimidating when nobody can tell what you're thinking. Eye contact is confident when it's steady and natural, and intimidating when it becomes a stare-down. Disagreement is confident when you say "I see it differently" and give a reason, and intimidating when it comes through eye rolls, silence or interruptions. Composure is confident when it's calm and warm, and intimidating when it's calm and cold. Every confident behaviour, she argues, has a warm version and a cold version. Presence lives in the warm one.

## What the research says

Second, the research she draws on. She cites Sylvia Ann Hewlett's well-known work on executive presence, which found that gravitas, meaning how you act, made up about two thirds of perceived presence, communication just over a quarter, and appearance only around five percent. She also cites work by Amy Cuddy and colleagues in the Harvard Business Review, arguing that people judge warmth before competence, so leaders should connect first and then lead.

And she raises an uncomfortable finding. A 2014 analysis by the linguist Kieran Snyder of around two hundred and fifty technology-sector performance reviews found that personality criticism, words like "abrasive", appeared far more often in reviews of women than of men. Vyas is careful to note it's one study, from one industry, more than a decade ago. But it suggests the line between confident and intimidating isn't drawn in the same place for everyone.

## Six unwritten rules

Third, her rules of presence, paraphrased. Listen before speaking, because the first person to speak is often the most nervous. Presence depends on how people feel, not on how much you talked. Nervousness rambles; confidence finishes the thought. Unnecessary apologies cost credibility. You can respect someone without agreeing with them. And warmth is a dial you adjust for each room, while confidence stays constant.

## Shrinking or overpowering?

Fourth, her two lists of warning signs. Signs you may be shrinking yourself: apologising before asking a question, ending statements with "if that makes sense?", filling every silence, agreeing in the meeting and disagreeing afterwards, and making yourself physically small. Signs you may have tipped into intimidating: people stop bringing you bad news, the team goes quiet when you walk in, you're described as "hard to read", and you defend yourself immediately when challenged. If three or more on the first list sound like you, take up more space. If three or more on the second list do, she says, adjust your warmth, not your confidence.

## The toolkit

Fifth, her practical habits. Count to three after you finish speaking, instead of rushing to fill the silence. Hold eye contact through a full thought. Ask yourself whether your comment moves the conversation forward before you make it. When you disagree, say calmly, "I see it differently", with one reason. And when someone disagrees with you, start with "Tell me more about that."

She also suggests deleting weak phrases. Instead of apologising before a question, just say "Quick question". Instead of "I think maybe we could possibly", say "I recommend". And instead of "Does that make sense?", ask "What questions do you have?"

Then there's the warmth dial. To sound more authoritative, slow down, use short sentences, and let your voice fall at the end of statements. To sound more approachable, smile, use people's names, ask follow-up questions, and share something personal. Turn warmth up with junior colleagues, first meetings and difficult feedback. Turn authority up with boards, in negotiations, and when you're the newest person in the room.

Finally, her intimidation check. Ask three people, a peer, someone who reports or used to report to you, and a senior colleague, how you come across and what makes it harder to disagree with you. Listen without defending yourself. If all three say the same thing, it's real. If only one does, it's data.

## Presence changes with seniority

She closes with an idea drawn from a recent Harvard Business Review article by the executive coach Amii Barnard-Bahn, about how presence can backfire for senior leaders who act as the expert in every room. Vyas's takeaway is elegant: early in your career, presence means taking up space. Later, it means making space for others.

## Recap

To recap. Deepali Vyas says confident and intimidating behaviour often look identical; the difference is whether people leave feeling seen or feeling small. Keep your confidence constant, and adjust your warmth to the room. Cut the apologies and hedges, finish your thoughts, and invite disagreement. And as you become more senior, presence shifts from taking up space to making space.
