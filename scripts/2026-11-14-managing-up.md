# Managing up

The relationship with your boss is probably the single most important working relationship you have. It shapes your projects, your reputation, your ratings and your promotions. Yet most people treat it as something that simply happens to them. In this episode, we'll look at managing up: what it really means, the classic research behind it, and practical ways to work well with a boss who is overloaded, a micromanager, or simply hard to read.

## What managing up really means

First, a misconception. Managing up sounds like flattery, or manipulating your boss. It isn't. In 1980, John Gabarro and John Kotter of Harvard Business School published an article in the Harvard Business Review called "Managing Your Boss". It became one of the magazine's most reprinted classics. At the time, the idea seemed almost illegitimate. Bosses managed people, not the other way round. But their research found that effective managers deliberately managed the relationship with their own boss, and that this was crucial to their success.

Their definition was simple: managing your boss means consciously working with your superior to get the best possible results for you, your boss, and the company. It's a relationship between two imperfect people who depend on each other. Your boss needs your work, information and support. You need their decisions, resources and backing.

## Understand your boss and yourself

Second, Gabarro and Kotter's core advice: understand both your boss and yourself.

Start with your boss. What are their goals and pressures? What is their own boss asking of them? In pharma, your manager may be under intense pressure from a submission timeline, a budget cut or a reorganisation that you only hear about indirectly. Understanding that context changes how you interpret their behaviour.

Then their working style. A key question is how your boss likes to receive information. Some people are readers. They want a written summary before a meeting so they can think. Others are listeners. They want you to talk them through it, and they'll never read a long email. Some want every detail. Others want only the conclusion and the decision they need to make. Bringing a fifteen-page analysis to a listener, or an unprepared verbal update to a reader, wastes everyone's time.

Notice how they make decisions too. Do they decide quickly, or do they need time and data? Do they want options, or a recommendation?

Then turn the lens on yourself. What are your own strengths, weaknesses and habits? Do you react badly to authority? Do you avoid bringing bad news? Do you need more autonomy than your boss naturally gives? Self-awareness is half of managing up.

## Practical habits

Third, practical habits that build a strong relationship.

Agree on expectations explicitly. Don't guess what success looks like. Ask: "What are the three things that matter most to you from me this quarter?" Then check in regularly, because priorities change.

Bring solutions, not just problems. When something goes wrong, come with the problem, its impact, and one or two options. "The statistics outputs are delayed by a week. That puts the report at risk. I suggest we start the review on the completed sections now. Do you agree?" That's very different from "We have a problem."

No surprises. Bad news travels best early and directly from you. A boss who finds out about a problem from someone else loses trust fast.

Manage their time. Your boss's time and attention are scarce. Batch non-urgent questions. Prepare for one-to-ones with a short agenda. Make it easy for them to help you.

Make them look good, honestly. Help your boss succeed on what they care about. Share information that helps them in their own meetings. That's not sycophancy. It's being a valuable team member.

## Difficult bosses

Fourth, the hard cases.

The overloaded boss. They're always in meetings and reply late. Keep your updates short, put the decision you need in the first line of every email, and propose a default: "Unless I hear otherwise by Thursday, I'll proceed with option A." This keeps work moving without them becoming a bottleneck.

The micromanager. Micromanagement is often a sign of anxiety or lack of trust. Reduce the anxiety by over-communicating at first: frequent short updates, early sight of drafts. As trust builds, propose a looser rhythm: "Would a weekly summary work instead of daily updates?"

The absent boss. They give you little guidance. Write your own objectives, share them, and ask for confirmation. Document what you've agreed. Absent bosses often appreciate people who take initiative.

The boss you simply clash with. Focus on shared goals. Separate their style from their intentions. And if the relationship really doesn't work, build relationships with other senior leaders, use a mentor for perspective, and consider whether a skip-level conversation or an internal move makes sense. Not every boss relationship can be fixed, and protecting your career sometimes means moving on.

## What to watch next

Looking ahead, hybrid work makes managing up both harder and more important. With fewer informal moments, the deliberate habits, like clear agendas, written updates and explicit expectations, carry more of the relationship.

## Recap

To recap. Managing up isn't flattery. It's consciously building a relationship that works for you, your boss and the company. Understand your boss's goals, pressures and preferred style, and understand yourself. Agree on expectations, bring solutions, avoid surprises, and respect their time. And adapt your approach for overloaded, micromanaging or absent bosses.
